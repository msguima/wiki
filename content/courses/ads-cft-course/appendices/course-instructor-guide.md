# Teaching the complete algebraic holography course

Edition 2.2, 1 October 2026. The 32 notes form a written draft for 30 teaching weeks: 60 lecture hours in Semester I and 45 in Semester II, with a separate weekly problem session if available. These allocations have not been rehearsed. The opening four lectures retain their [[opening-block-instructor-guide|detailed meeting guide]].

The pedagogical reference is the canonical AQFT course. Preserve its distinction between a calculation students can reconstruct, the fuller instructor account, and a research extension. A stated theorem is not a failed proof: it becomes a problem only when students cannot identify its hypotheses or how it is used.

## The recurring classroom method

Begin by asking students to identify the system, state, and accessible algebra. Ask for a prediction before introducing the main formula. Work the first nontrivial case at the board, pausing where a trace, a support condition, a regulator, or a change of representation enters. Finish by asking what additional input would be needed for a geometric interpretation.

For a four-hour unit, use two meetings. A provisional first meeting is 15 minutes of retrieval, 25 of setup, 40 of calculation, 20 of a paired check, and 20 of discussion. The second can use 15 minutes of reconstruction, 45 of the next calculation, 25 of interpretation, 25 of problems, and 10 of synthesis. Adjust this after teaching; the numbers are a preparation tool, not evidence that a dense derivation fits.

For a three-hour unit, a possible allocation is 15 minutes of retrieval, 35 of setup, 45 of the worked example, a 10-minute break, 30 of interpretation, 30 of problems, and 15 of synthesis. Do not sacrifice the worked example to cover every optional paragraph.

Lectures 5–7 retain the longer routed lists used in the opening dossier. Lectures 8–32, rewritten to the AQFT standard, have fifteen routed problems each: six core, six self-study and three research extensions. Their answer checkpoints expose the decisive step but leave room for students to write full solutions.

Deferring a theorem's proof does not remove its hypotheses from class. In Lecture 22, for example, students should explicitly identify the all-balls assumption, the two dictionary entries, and the analyticity hypothesis of the injectivity step, which the note proves only for static perturbations of AdS$_3$. Otherwise the argument will sound like an elementary proof of nonlinear Einstein dynamics, which it is not. In Lectures 25–27 the corresponding list is the transparent coupling, the choice of matter state, the island rule itself, and, in Lecture 27, replica symmetry and the continuation in $n$; the free-fermion formula of Lecture 26 is exact for that matter and its late-time limit holds for any conformal field theory. In Lectures 28–31 it is Haag duality, strong additivity and factoriality in the three-arc proposition, the vacuum in the conformal Bisognano–Wichmann theorem, the KMS state and the factor property in the proposition of Lecture 29, the common cyclic and separating vector and the sign of the compression in Wiesbrock's theorem, and, in Lecture 31, the limit $G_N\to0$ and the Hartle–Hawking state.

## Bridges for students with strong quantum mechanics and information theory

Use partial trace as the first example of restriction, then explain why a functional remains meaningful when a regional trace does not. Use the effective thermal oscillator to introduce an entanglement spectrum before discussing type III. Use the classical recovery channel before the Petz map. Use changes of Schmidt spectrum before graph cuts. Use two ordinary affine maps before abstract modular inclusions.

Repeat an example when the mathematical language changes. The qutrit decoder reappears in entropy, modular flow, distinguishability, approximate recovery, and the final synthesis. Students should recognize the same calculation in a more general setting.

In gravity units, draw the distinction between a fixed-background wave equation, a boundary dictionary, and a gravitational field equation. The scalar calculation belongs to the first two. The entanglement-first-law argument adds the third under stated assumptions.

## Four points that need deliberate discussion

**Type III.** Finite $N$ does not mean finite-dimensional Hilbert space. Continuum local algebras and emergent algebras of selected large-$N$ observables are different constructions. The tracial infinite-product counterexample should remain in the classroom route.

**Reconstruction.** Equality of one expectation value is weaker than an operator intertwiner. Tests on basis-state populations miss quantum coherence. Near a surface transition, the reconstructed algebra and the chosen code family need to be specified together.

**Islands.** The written JT example is static at zero temperature and thermal in its two-sided extension. Its thermal entropy saturates; it is not a falling evaporation curve. Its extremum is spatially minimizing and temporally maximizing. Ask students to show both facts.

**Emergent time.** An affine group is an exact algebraic output under the theorem's hypotheses. A null-coordinate interpretation and an infalling proper-time interpretation are different additional claims. The one-particle group representation alone does not provide a cyclic separating vacuum and a net.

## Review before classroom use

Have the instructor or a peer reconstruct the scalar counterterm calculation, the time-dependent island endpoint variation, and the modular-inclusion sign conventions without consulting the answers. Try a representative meeting with the intended audience. Record which prerequisite required repair and which material was deferred.

The validation record separates executable checks from mathematical exposition and teaching readiness. A clean reader and passing finite-model checks are useful evidence; they do not substitute for a first teaching trial.
