# Week 2 — Von Neumann Algebras and the Double Commutant

> *We strengthened "Banach algebra" to "C\*-algebra" by adding one axiom (the C\*-identity) and discovered enormous structural rigidity. We now strengthen "C\*-algebra" to "von Neumann algebra" by changing the topology in which we close. The cost: vN algebras come with a representation built in. The benefit: a wealth of projections, a topology in which the bicommutant theorem trivializes the algebra-vs-topology distinction, and the foundation for everything in modular theory and beyond.*

## 1. Why a new closure

The C\*-algebras of [[week-01-cstar-algebras-and-gns|Week 1]] are *abstract*: they can be defined without reference to any Hilbert space. Once we fix a state $\omega$ and run the GNS construction, we obtain a concrete realization $\pi_\omega(\mathcal{A}) \subset \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_\omega)$. But this realization is a \*-subalgebra closed in the *norm* topology — not in any of the weaker operator topologies.

Why does this matter? Three reasons.

**(i) Spectral projections are missing.** Suppose $a$ is a self-adjoint element of a C\*-algebra $\mathcal{A}$ and $E \subset \mathbb{R}$ is a Borel set. The spectral projection $1_E(a)$ — the projection onto the spectral subspace where $a$ takes values in $E$ — is in general **not** in $\mathcal{A}$. It is only the limit, in the strong operator topology, of polynomials in $a$. Concretely: in $\mathcal{A} = C([0,1])$, the polynomial functions in $a(x) = x$ are dense in $C([0,1])$ in sup-norm, but the indicator functions $1_{[0, 1/2]}$ are *not* in $C([0,1])$. We need a coarser topology — one that allows pointwise limits of bounded sequences of polynomials — to recover them.

Without spectral projections we cannot define yes/no measurements, decompose states into pure components, or develop the basic measurement theory of operator algebras.

**(ii) Cyclic representations need not be irreducible at the C\*-level.** Even when $\pi_\omega(\mathcal{A})$ acts irreducibly on $\mathcal{H}_\omega$ in the sense that the only invariant closed subspaces are $\{0\}$ and $\mathcal{H}_\omega$, there can be operators in $\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_\omega)$ that *commute with $\pi_\omega(\mathcal{A})$* yet are not in $\pi_\omega(\mathcal{A})$ itself. We want the *full* commutation relationship to be visible.

**(iii) Many constructions are intrinsically WOT-closed.** In QFT we will eventually want to consider the algebra generated by *Weyl operators with test functions in a region* (Block C). This generation is intrinsically a topological closure operation, and the right topology is WOT (or, equivalently, SOT on bounded sets) because spectral projections and indicator functions enter naturally.

The remedy is to enlarge $\pi_\omega(\mathcal{A})$ to a larger algebra by closing in a weaker topology. The right one — the *unique* one with the structural properties below — is the weak operator topology.

## 2. Operator topologies on $\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$

Recall that $\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$ admits at least four useful topologies:

1. **Norm topology**: $T_n \to T$ if $\|T_n - T\| \to 0$.
2. **Strong operator topology (SOT)**: $T_n \to T$ if $T_n \xi \to T\xi$ in norm for every $\xi \in \mathcal{H}$.
3. **Weak operator topology (WOT)**: $T_n \to T$ if $\langle\eta, T_n\xi\rangle \to \langle\eta, T\xi\rangle$ for every $\xi, \eta \in \mathcal{H}$.
4. **σ-weak topology** (also called *ultraweak*): $T_n \to T$ if $\sum_i \langle\eta_i, T_n\xi_i\rangle \to \sum_i \langle\eta_i, T\xi_i\rangle$ for every pair of sequences $\{\xi_i\}, \{\eta_i\}$ with $\sum \|\xi_i\|^2 < \infty, \sum \|\eta_i\|^2 < \infty$.

Norm convergence implies SOT, which implies WOT. The σ-weak agrees with WOT on bounded sets (because the SOT-bounded sets are equicontinuous — Banach–Steinhaus) but is in general finer.

> **Physical picture.** Each topology answers a different operational question about when two operators are "close." Norm closeness is *uniform* closeness: $a_n \to a$ in norm means the worst-case difference in expectation values, over all states, goes to zero. That is an extremely demanding notion — no experiment ever probes all states at once. WOT closeness is closeness of *matrix elements* $\langle\eta, a_n \xi\rangle$, i.e. of transition amplitudes between specific states: $a_n \to a$ in WOT means every correlation function you could actually measure converges. SOT is intermediate: the action on each fixed state vector converges. The physically meaningful closure is the weak one — if no conceivable experiment in the given representation can distinguish a limit operator from the algebra, the operator should be counted as an observable. That is precisely why spectral projections (yes/no questions about an observable) belong to the vN closure even when they are missing from the C\*-algebra: they are WOT-limits of things we can measure, hence measurable in the limit.

**Lemma 2.1. [Proved.]** *In finite dimensions ($\dim\mathcal{H} = n < \infty$), all four topologies coincide.*

**Proof.** $\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}) \cong M_n(\mathbb{C})$ is finite-dimensional. Any two norms on a finite-dimensional vector space are equivalent (a standard linear-algebra result). The SOT and WOT are induced by families of seminorms, and on a finite-dimensional space they all reduce to the same norm topology. $\square$

In infinite dimensions, the topologies are genuinely distinct. The key examples:

**Example 2.2 (Distinct topologies in $\mathcal{B}(\ell^2)$).** Let $S$ be the unilateral shift on $\ell^2$: $S e_n = e_{n+1}$. Consider the sequence $S^n$.
- *Norm:* $\|S^n\| = 1$ for all $n$, so $S^n$ does not converge in norm.
- *SOT:* $S^n \xi \to 0$ for every $\xi \in \ell^2$? The component of $\xi$ at index $k$ is shifted to index $k+n$, which goes off to infinity. The norm of $S^n \xi$ remains $\|\xi\|$. So $S^n \xi$ does *not* converge to anything in norm — the SOT-limit doesn't exist.
- However, $\langle \eta, S^n \xi\rangle = \sum_k \overline{\eta_{k+n}} \xi_k \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$ by Cauchy–Schwarz on the tail of $\eta$. So $S^n \to 0$ in *WOT* but not in SOT.

**Lemma 2.3. [Sketched — separate continuity proved; non-joint via WOT counterexample.]** *On $\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$, multiplication is jointly continuous in the norm topology, but only **separately** continuous in SOT and WOT in general.*

**WOT counterexample to joint continuity.** Let $S$ be the unilateral shift on $\ell^2$. As in Example 2.2, $S^n \to 0$ and $S^{*n} \to 0$ in WOT (the matrix elements $\langle e_j, S^n e_k\rangle$ vanish for $n$ large enough). Yet
$$
S^{*n} \cdot S^n = 1 \quad \text{for every } n \ge 0,
$$
since $S^*$ is a left inverse of $S$. So $A_n := S^{*n}$ and $B_n := S^n$ satisfy $A_n \to 0$, $B_n \to 0$ in WOT, but $A_n B_n = 1 \not\to 0 = 0 \cdot 0$. Joint WOT-continuity of multiplication therefore fails.

(*Note on SOT.* In SOT, multiplication actually *is* jointly continuous on bounded sets — this follows from $\|(A_n B_n - AB)\xi\| \le \|A_n\| \|(B_n - B)\xi\| + \|(A_n - A)(B\xi)\|$ when $\|A_n\|$ is bounded. SOT joint continuity fails only on unbounded sets, where the counterexamples are more delicate. For this course the WOT failure above is the operationally relevant one.)

**Proof of separate continuity.** Suppose $T_n \to T$ in WOT and $S \in \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$ is fixed. Then for any $\xi, \eta$,
$$\langle \eta, T_n S \xi\rangle = \langle \eta, T_n (S\xi)\rangle \to \langle \eta, T(S\xi)\rangle = \langle\eta, TS\xi\rangle,$$
so $T_n S \to TS$ in WOT. The other side is similar (use $T_n^* \to T^*$ in WOT, which follows from the definition since $\langle\xi, T_n^*\eta\rangle = \overline{\langle\eta, T_n\xi\rangle}$). $\square$

The non-joint continuity of multiplication is the technical reason vN algebra theory requires care: products of converging sequences need not converge, and many "obvious" identity manipulations fail. This is why the bicommutant theorem (which sidesteps this issue) is so important.

## 3. Von Neumann algebras: definition

**Definition 3.1.** A *von Neumann algebra* on a Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$ is a unital \*-subalgebra $\mathcal{M} \subset \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$ that is closed in the weak operator topology.

We will see (Theorem 4.1) that this is equivalent to closure in SOT, σ-weak, and several other topologies — but *not* the norm topology. A norm-closed unital \*-subalgebra of $\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$ is called a *concrete C\*-algebra*; a vN algebra is "more closed" than that.

**Definition 3.2.** The *commutant* of a subset $S \subset \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$ is
$$
S' := \{ T \in \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}) : Ta = aT \text{ for all } a \in S \}.
$$

> **Physical picture.** The commutant is the set of all operations *compatible* with $S$: everything that can be done to the system without disturbing any measurement of the observables in $S$. Two readings will recur. (1) *Subsystem reading:* if $S$ is the algebra of one subsystem, $S'$ contains the operators of "everything else" — in the bipartite model $\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_1)\otimes 1$, the commutant is exactly $1 \otimes \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_2)$ (Example 5.4). (2) *Relativistic reading:* in AQFT, microcausality says the algebra of a spacelike-separated region sits inside the commutant of the local algebra; Haag duality (Block C) is the statement that it *exhausts* the commutant — the causal complement is everything compatible. The commutant thus turns a kinematical notion (commutation) into a structural one (a second algebra), and the interplay $\mathcal{M} \leftrightarrow \mathcal{M}'$ becomes, after Tomita–Takesaki (Week 5), a precise system–environment symmetry implemented by the modular conjugation $J$.

**Lemma 3.3. [Proved.]** *The commutant $S'$ of any subset is a unital algebra closed in WOT (hence a vN algebra when $S$ is self-adjoint, i.e., $S = S^*$).*

**Proof.** Closure under sum, scalar multiplication, and product is by linearity of commutation. For closure under adjoint when $S^* = S$: if $T \in S'$ and $a \in S$, then $a^* \in S$ (because $S$ is self-adjoint), so $T a^* = a^* T$. Taking adjoints, $a T^* = T^* a$ for all $a \in S$, i.e., $T^* \in S'$. Closure in WOT: if $T_n \to T$ in WOT and $T_n \in S'$, then for any $a \in S$,
$$\langle \eta, (Ta - aT)\xi\rangle = \lim_n \langle\eta, (T_n a - a T_n)\xi\rangle = 0,$$
so $Ta = aT$, i.e., $T \in S'$. $\square$

The double commutant $S'' := (S')'$ is therefore also a vN algebra (when $S$ is self-adjoint). And $S \subset S''$ trivially (if $a \in S$ commutes with everything in $S'$, then $a \in S''$), so $S \subset S''$ for every self-adjoint $S$.

## 4. The bicommutant theorem

**Theorem 4.1 (von Neumann's bicommutant theorem, 1929). [Proved in finite dim §4.1; sketched via Kaplansky in general — refs: Bratteli–Robinson Vol. I §2.4.3.]** *Let $\mathcal{M}$ be a unital \*-subalgebra of $\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$. The following are equivalent:*

*(i) $\mathcal{M} = \mathcal{M}''$;*
*(ii) $\mathcal{M}$ is closed in WOT;*
*(iii) $\mathcal{M}$ is closed in SOT;*
*(iv) $\mathcal{M}$ is closed in the σ-weak topology.*

This is the foundational theorem of von Neumann algebra theory: it identifies a *purely algebraic* condition (being equal to its double commutant) with a *purely topological* condition (being closed in WOT/SOT/σ-weak). It is what makes the theory tractable — to check that a concrete \*-subalgebra is a vN algebra, we can use either characterization, whichever is easier in context.

> **Physical picture.** The bicommutant theorem is a statement about what counts as an observable of a system once its basic observables are given. Start from a set $S$ of operators you declare measurable. The commutant $S'$ is the set of all symmetries and external operations compatible with $S$. The theorem says: the operators obtainable from $S$ by *taking limits of measurements* (topological closure) are exactly the operators that *respect every compatibility that $S$ respects* (the double commutant). "What you can approximate by experiments on the system" = "what commutes with everything the system is independent of." Neither side mentions the other's structure — one is analysis, the other is algebra — and their identity is the non-trivial content. This duality is also why the construction $S \mapsto S''$ is the right notion of "the algebra generated by $S$": it adds no operator that could be excluded on grounds of locality or compatibility.

### 4.1 Proof in the finite-dimensional case

The finite-dimensional case is the prototype and we work it out in full.

**Setup.** Let $\dim\mathcal{H} = n < \infty$. By Lemma 2.1, all topologies coincide; "closed" is automatic for any subspace. The non-trivial content of the theorem is then: *every unital \*-subalgebra of $M_n(\mathbb{C})$ equals its own double commutant.*

**Lemma 4.2 (Wedderburn). [Stated only — refs: any standard text on representation theory of finite-dimensional semisimple algebras (Maschke + Schur), e.g. Lang, *Algebra*, §XVII.]** *Let $\mathcal{M} \subset M_n(\mathbb{C})$ be a unital \*-subalgebra. Then there is an orthogonal decomposition*
$$
\mathcal{H} = \bigoplus_{i=1}^k \mathcal{H}_i, \quad \mathcal{H}_i \cong \mathbb{C}^{m_i} \otimes \mathbb{C}^{k_i},
$$
*such that, with respect to this decomposition,*
$$
\mathcal{M} \cong \bigoplus_{i=1}^k M_{m_i}(\mathbb{C}) \otimes 1_{k_i}
$$
*— $\mathcal{M}$ acts as $a_i \otimes 1$ on $\mathcal{H}_i$ for some $a_i \in M_{m_i}(\mathbb{C})$, and the $a_i$ are arbitrary.*

We do not prove Wedderburn here; it follows from the basic representation theory of finite-dimensional semisimple algebras over $\mathbb{C}$ (Maschke + Schur). The key structural takeaway: any unital \*-subalgebra of $M_n(\mathbb{C})$ is determined by a list of pairs $\{(m_i, k_i)\}$ with $\sum m_i k_i = n$.

**Computing the commutant.** Given the Wedderburn decomposition, we compute $\mathcal{M}'$:
$$
T \in \mathcal{M}' \iff T \text{ commutes with all } a \otimes 1 \text{ on each } \mathcal{H}_i.
$$
On $\mathcal{H}_i = \mathbb{C}^{m_i} \otimes \mathbb{C}^{k_i}$, an operator commuting with all $M_{m_i}(\mathbb{C}) \otimes 1$ must (by Schur's lemma in tensor form) be of the form $1 \otimes b_i$ for some $b_i \in M_{k_i}(\mathbb{C})$. Hence
$$
\mathcal{M}' \cong \bigoplus_{i=1}^k 1_{m_i} \otimes M_{k_i}(\mathbb{C}).
$$
And by symmetry, applying the same calculation to $\mathcal{M}'$,
$$
\mathcal{M}'' \cong \bigoplus_{i=1}^k M_{m_i}(\mathbb{C}) \otimes 1_{k_i} = \mathcal{M}.
$$
This proves the bicommutant theorem in finite dimensions. $\square$

The proof is satisfying because it makes vN algebra theory in finite dimensions completely explicit: a vN subalgebra of $M_n(\mathbb{C})$ is just a direct-sum-of-matrix-algebras structure determined by the dimensions $\{m_i, k_i\}$. The commutant comes for free by switching the roles of "intrinsic" and "multiplicity" indices.

#### Worked example: the diagonal algebra in $M_3(\mathbb{C})$

To make the bicommutant theorem concrete, take $\mathcal{M} \subset M_3(\mathbb{C})$ to be the algebra of *diagonal* matrices:
$$
\mathcal{M} = \big\{\,\mathrm{diag}(\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3) \,:\, \alpha_i \in \mathbb{C}\,\big\} \;\cong\; \mathbb{C} \oplus \mathbb{C} \oplus \mathbb{C}.
$$
This is the Wedderburn decomposition $\bigoplus_{i=1}^3 M_1(\mathbb{C}) \otimes 1_{1}$ (three blocks, each $1 \times 1$, with no multiplicity).

**Computing $\mathcal{M}'$.** A matrix $T = (T_{ij}) \in M_3(\mathbb{C})$ commutes with every diagonal $\mathrm{diag}(\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3)$ iff $T_{ij}\alpha_j = \alpha_i T_{ij}$ for all $i, j$ and all choices of $\alpha_k$. For $i \neq j$, choosing $\alpha_j = 1, \alpha_i = 0$ forces $T_{ij} = 0$. So $\mathcal{M}' = \mathcal{M}$ — the diagonal algebra is its own commutant. (This is the prototype "maximal abelian subalgebra," to be developed in §7 for arbitrary spectra.)

**Computing $\mathcal{M}''$.** Since $\mathcal{M}' = \mathcal{M}$, the double commutant is $\mathcal{M}'' = (\mathcal{M}')' = \mathcal{M}' = \mathcal{M}$. So $\mathcal{M} = \mathcal{M}''$, confirming the bicommutant theorem in this case.

**Comparison: a non-trivial multiplicity.** Now take $\mathcal{N} \subset M_3(\mathbb{C})$ given by
$$
\mathcal{N} = \big\{\,\mathrm{diag}(\alpha, \alpha, \beta) \,:\, \alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{C}\,\big\} \;\cong\; \mathbb{C} \oplus \mathbb{C}.
$$
This is the Wedderburn decomposition $M_1(\mathbb{C}) \otimes 1_2 \;\oplus\; M_1(\mathbb{C}) \otimes 1_1$ — two blocks, the first with multiplicity 2, the second with multiplicity 1.

A computation similar to above gives
$$
\mathcal{N}' = \big\{\, \begin{pmatrix} A & 0 \\ 0 & \gamma\end{pmatrix} \,:\, A \in M_2(\mathbb{C}),\; \gamma \in \mathbb{C}\,\big\} \;\cong\; M_2(\mathbb{C}) \oplus \mathbb{C}.
$$
The multiplicity-2 block in $\mathcal{N}$ allows $\mathcal{N}'$ to "see" the full $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ acting on the corresponding two-dimensional subspace. The multiplicity-1 block leaves only $\mathbb{C}$ in the commutant.

Computing $\mathcal{N}''$: take the commutant of $M_2(\mathbb{C}) \oplus \mathbb{C}$ inside $M_3$. This is $\mathbb{C} \cdot 1_2 \oplus \mathbb{C} = \mathcal{N}$. So again $\mathcal{N}'' = \mathcal{N}$, and the bicommutant theorem is verified.

The contrast between $\mathcal{M}$ (no multiplicity) and $\mathcal{N}$ (multiplicity 2 in the first block) shows the role of the $k_i$ indices in Wedderburn: they are exactly the dimensions of the commutant blocks. Algebra and commutant are dual, with intrinsic and multiplicity dimensions swapping roles.

### 4.2 Sketch of the infinite-dimensional case

The general proof requires *Kaplansky's density theorem*. We state it as a black box:

**Theorem 4.3 (Kaplansky 1951). [Stated only — refs: Kaplansky, Ann. of Math. 53 (1951) 235–249; Bratteli–Robinson Vol. I §2.4.3.]** *For any unital \*-subalgebra $\mathcal{M}_0 \subset \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$, the unit ball $\{a \in \mathcal{M}_0'' : \|a\| \le 1\}$ is the SOT-closure of the unit ball $\{a \in \mathcal{M}_0 : \|a\| \le 1\}$.*

Given Kaplansky, the bicommutant theorem follows: any element $a \in \mathcal{M}''$ can be approximated in SOT by a sequence of elements in $\mathcal{M}$ of bounded norm. Hence $\mathcal{M}$, if SOT-closed, contains $\mathcal{M}''$. The reverse inclusion $\mathcal{M} \subset \mathcal{M}''$ is automatic.

The proof of Kaplansky uses *polar decomposition* and *continuous functional calculus*: given $a \in \mathcal{M}_0''$, the elements $a, a^*, a a^*, |a|, $ etc. are all in $\mathcal{M}_0''$, and one truncates them using $f(|a|)$ for suitable $f \in C[0, \|a\|]$ (which is in the C\*-closure of the polynomials). The truncations are SOT-approximable by elements of $\mathcal{M}_0$.

The cost: we need the C\*-closure of $\mathcal{M}_0$ to have the continuous functional calculus, which requires the C\*-identity. This is one place where the C\*-structure (Week 1) is genuinely used to do the vN algebra theory of Week 2.

### 4.3 Why bicommutant matters

The practical consequence of Theorem 4.1 is that we have two complementary ways to describe vN algebras:

- **Topologically:** as WOT-closed unital \*-subalgebras.
- **Algebraically:** as $S''$ for some self-adjoint subset $S$.

The second description is constructive: given any "interesting" set of operators, the vN algebra they generate is just $S''$. For example, the vN algebra $W^*(a)$ generated by a single self-adjoint operator $a$ is $\{a\}''$, and we will compute it explicitly in §7 below.

## 5. Examples and key constructions

**Example 5.1 ($\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$).** $\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$ is itself a vN algebra (trivially: it is its own SOT-closure). Its commutant is the *center* of $\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$, which is $\mathbb{C}\cdot 1$: the only operators that commute with *all* bounded operators are scalar multiples of the identity. Hence $\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$ is a *factor*:

**Definition 5.2.** A vN algebra $\mathcal{M}$ is a *factor* if its center $\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{M}) := \mathcal{M} \cap \mathcal{M}'$ is trivial: $\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{M}) = \mathbb{C}\cdot 1$.

Factors are the "irreducible building blocks" of vN algebra theory: the type classification (Week 3) is a classification of factors. Every vN algebra is a "direct integral" of factors over its center — a structural fact we will use without proving.

> **Physical picture.** The center is the *classical part* of the theory: central elements commute with every observable, so they can be measured without disturbing anything — they behave like classical parameters, not quantum observables. Nontrivial center means *superselection sectors*: the value of a central observable (total electric charge, a θ-angle, the phase of a system across a phase transition) is fixed within each sector, and no physical operation creates superpositions across sectors. A factor is a theory with no such classical labels — fully quantum, no superselection. The direct-integral decomposition over the center is then the precise statement that *any* quantum system is a classical mixture of fully quantum ones: first diagonalize the classical data, then study each fixed-classical-value world separately. This is why classifying factors classifies everything.

**Example 5.3 ($L^\infty(X, \mu)$ as a maximal abelian vN algebra).** Let $(X, \mu)$ be a $\sigma$-finite measure space, and let $\mathcal{H} = L^2(X, \mu)$. Then $L^\infty(X, \mu)$ acts on $\mathcal{H}$ by multiplication: $(M_f \xi)(x) = f(x) \xi(x)$.

**Claim.** $L^\infty(X, \mu)' = L^\infty(X, \mu)$ (this algebra is its own commutant).

**Proof of claim.** Suppose $T \in L^\infty(X, \mu)'$. We show $T = M_g$ for some $g \in L^\infty$.

Pick a measurable $E \subset X$ with $\mu(E) < \infty$ (uses $\sigma$-finiteness) and set $\xi_E = 1_E \in L^2$. Define $g_E := T \xi_E$, an element of $L^2$. For any $f \in L^\infty$ with $\mathrm{supp}(f) \subset E$,
$$
T(f \xi_E) = T(M_f \xi_E) = M_f T(\xi_E) = M_f g_E = f g_E.
$$
But also, $f \xi_E = f \cdot 1_E = f$ (since $\mathrm{supp}(f) \subset E$). So $T f = f g_E$ for every such $f$.

By exhausting $X$ with $\sigma$-finite increasing sets $\{E_n\}$, the functions $g_{E_n}$ are consistent, defining a measurable $g$ on $X$ with $T f = M_g f$ for all $f \in L^2$ with bounded support. This extends by density to $L^2$. The boundedness of $T$ as an operator on $L^2$ implies $g \in L^\infty$ with $\|g\|_\infty = \|T\|$. Hence $T = M_g \in L^\infty(X, \mu)$. $\square$

The maximality $L^\infty(X, \mu)' = L^\infty(X, \mu)$ is what makes commutative vN algebras tractable: they coincide with their commutants, hence their double commutants — they are vN algebras automatically. This is the deep connection between *measure theory* and *vN algebra theory*: every commutative vN algebra is $L^\infty$ of some measure space (Gelfand–Naimark for vN), and conversely.

> **Physical picture.** This example fixes the dictionary that runs the whole subject: commutative vN algebra = classical probability theory in operator clothing. $L^\infty(X,\mu)$ is the algebra of bounded random variables; normal states on it are probability densities ($L^1$ functions, Example 6.4); spectral projections are events; the lattice of projections is the σ-algebra. Von Neumann algebra theory is then precisely *noncommutative measure theory* — the slogan is exact, not loose: drop commutativity from $L^\infty$ and you get vN algebras; drop it from $C(X)$ and you get C\*-algebras (noncommutative topology). Week 3's type classification answers the question this dictionary immediately raises: what kinds of "noncommutative measure spaces" exist that have no classical counterpart? Types II and III are the answer.

**Example 5.4 (Tensor product commutant theorem).** Let $\mathcal{H} = \mathcal{H}_1 \otimes \mathcal{H}_2$, and let $\mathcal{M}_i \subset \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_i)$ be vN algebras. Then
$$
(\mathcal{M}_1 \otimes \mathcal{M}_2)' = \mathcal{M}_1' \otimes \mathcal{M}_2',
$$
where the tensor product on the left is the WOT-closure of the algebraic tensor product. This is *Tomita's commutation theorem*.

In the special case $\mathcal{M}_1 = \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_1), \mathcal{M}_2 = 1$:
$$
(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_1) \otimes 1)' = 1 \otimes \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_2).
$$

**Proof in finite dimensions.** Take $\mathcal{H}_i = \mathbb{C}^{n_i}$. By Wedderburn (Lemma 4.2) applied to $\mathcal{M}_1 \otimes 1$, the commutant is $\mathcal{M}_1' \otimes \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_2)$. Iterating: $(\mathcal{M}_1 \otimes \mathcal{M}_2)' = \mathcal{M}_1' \otimes \mathcal{M}_2'$ by the same argument. $\square$

**Warning — what this theorem is *not* in AQFT.** The tensor commutant theorem above presumes a *spatial tensor-product structure* $\mathcal{H} = \mathcal{H}_1 \otimes \mathcal{H}_2$. This is the right model for finite-dimensional bipartite quantum systems and for type-I situations where the Hilbert space genuinely factors. *It is not the right model for AQFT in type III.*

In a relativistic QFT, when a Cauchy slice $\Sigma$ is decomposed into spacelike-disjoint regions $\Sigma_1, \Sigma_2$, the corresponding local algebras $\mathcal{A}(\Sigma_1), \mathcal{A}(\Sigma_2)$ satisfy *commutation* (microcausality / locality) and often *Haag duality* ($\mathcal{A}(\Sigma_1)' = \mathcal{A}(\Sigma_2)$ for an appropriate causal complement) — but the underlying Hilbert space generally does *not* factor as $\mathcal{H}_1 \otimes \mathcal{H}_2$. The local algebras are type III$_1$ (Block C); type III algebras admit no minimal projections, so the identification with operators-on-a-tensor-factor fails in a strong structural sense.

A *tensor* factorization of the algebra structure does emerge under the additional **split property** (Doplicher–Longo, Buchholz, Wichmann) and **nuclearity** assumptions, applied to algebras of well-separated regions. But this is a theorem requiring real hypotheses, not a corollary of the spatial commutant theorem above.

So: keep this theorem as a finite-dimensional / type-I model. In AQFT use locality, Haag duality, and (where applicable) the split property — not automatic tensor factorization.

**Example 5.5 (Group von Neumann algebras).** Let $G$ be a discrete group, and let $\lambda : G \to \mathcal{B}(\ell^2(G))$ be the left-regular representation: $(\lambda(g) \xi)(h) = \xi(g^{-1} h)$. The vN algebra
$$
\mathcal{L}(G) := \{\lambda(g) : g \in G\}'' \subset \mathcal{B}(\ell^2(G))
$$
is the *group vN algebra* of $G$. It is a fundamental construction with deep ties to representation theory.

**Properties:**
1. *$\mathcal{L}(G)$ admits a tracial vector state.* The trace is $\tau(a) = \langle \delta_e, a \delta_e\rangle$ where $\delta_e \in \ell^2(G)$ is the indicator of the identity element. Tracial: for any $g, h$, $\tau(\lambda(g)\lambda(h)) = \tau(\lambda(gh)) = [gh = e]$, while $\tau(\lambda(h)\lambda(g)) = [hg = e]$, and $gh = e \iff hg = e$. So $\tau$ is tracial on the linear span of $\{\lambda(g)\}$, hence on $\mathcal{L}(G)$.
2. *$\mathcal{L}(G)$ is a factor iff $G$ is icc* (every non-identity conjugacy class is infinite). Examples of icc: $S_\infty$ (finitary permutations), free groups $F_n$ for $n \ge 2$, $\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$.
3. *For abelian $G$,* $\mathcal{L}(G) \cong L^\infty(\hat G)$ (Pontryagin dual), via Fourier transform. So abelian group vN algebras are commutative — never factors (unless $G$ is trivial).
4. *For icc $G$,* $\mathcal{L}(G)$ is a type II$_1$ factor (proved in Week 3). This is one of the most flexible sources of type II$_1$ factors.

Group vN algebras are the backbone of the *theory of $\mathrm{II}_1$-factors* — Connes' classification of type III, the theory of property (T), and the rigidity results of Popa all start here.

**Example 5.6 (Spectral projections live in $\mathcal{M}$).** If $a \in \mathcal{M}$ is self-adjoint, every spectral projection $1_E(a)$ for Borel $E \subset \sigma(a)$ is in $\mathcal{M}$.

**Reason.** Polynomials $p_n(a)$ with $p_n \to 1_E$ in $L^2(\sigma(a), E_a)$ converge to $1_E(a)$ in SOT (this requires a small argument with the spectral theorem). Since $p_n(a) \in \mathcal{M}$ and $\mathcal{M}$ is SOT-closed, $1_E(a) \in \mathcal{M}$. $\square$

This is the crucial property the C\*-closure does *not* have, and the most immediately useful consequence of Theorem 4.1.

## 6. The predual

**Definition 6.1.** A linear functional $\omega : \mathcal{M} \to \mathbb{C}$ is *normal* if it is continuous in the σ-weak topology (equivalently: continuous on bounded sets in WOT).

A non-normal state corresponds to a "pathological" measurement — typically, a state on $\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$ defined via an ultrafilter or other axiom-of-choice construction. Such states are physically irrelevant but mathematically real; the requirement of normality excludes them.

> **Physical picture.** Normality is the noncommutative version of *countable additivity* in probability theory. A normal state respects limits: if a sequence of yes/no questions (projections) increases to a question $p$, the probability assigned to $p$ is the limit of the probabilities — exactly as a σ-additive measure treats an increasing union of events. A non-normal state is like a finitely-additive-but-not-σ-additive "measure": internally consistent, but it assigns probabilities no sequence of experiments can ever exhibit, because it is discontinuous against the limits experiments actually take. Restricting to normal states is therefore not a technical convenience but an operational axiom: *physically preparable states produce statistics that converge when measurements converge.* In Week 4 and beyond, "faithful normal state" is the standing hypothesis of modular theory — normality is what ties the modular objects to the weak topologies in which QFT limits are taken.

**Theorem 6.2 (Sakai). [Stated only — refs: Sakai, *C\*-algebras and W\*-algebras*, ch. 1.]** *Every vN algebra $\mathcal{M}$ has a unique Banach space of normal linear functionals, called the predual $\mathcal{M}_*$, such that $\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{M}_*)^*$ as Banach spaces.*

This is the abstract characterization of vN algebras: $\mathcal{M}$ is a vN algebra iff it is a C\*-algebra that is the dual of *some* Banach space. (Sakai's theorem, 1956.) It tells us that vN algebras are *intrinsically* defined — we don't need to fix a specific Hilbert-space realization to know that $\mathcal{M}$ is "abstractly" a vN algebra.

**Example 6.3.** $\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})_* = \mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})$ (trace-class operators), and the duality is $\omega_\rho(a) = \mathrm{Tr}(\rho a)$ for $\rho$ trace-class. Normal states on $\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$ are exactly density-matrix states. Non-normal states do exist (as functionals on $\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$) but they are not represented by any $\rho \in \mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})$.

**Example 6.4.** $L^\infty(X, \mu)_* = L^1(X, \mu)$, with the duality $\omega_f(g) = \int f g \, d\mu$. Normal states are absolutely continuous probability measures.

**Example 6.5.** For the hyperfinite II$_1$ factor $\mathcal{R}$ (Week 3), the predual $\mathcal{R}_*$ is more subtle — it consists of functionals $\omega(a) = \tau(\rho a)$ for "noncommutative $L^1$" densities $\rho$. The trace $\tau$ itself is a normal state (in fact the trace), and $\mathcal{R}_*$ is built around it.

The predual is the natural "state space" of a vN algebra: it contains the physically meaningful states and allows one to talk about convergence of states in a reasonable sense.

## 7. Worked computation: the vN algebra of a self-adjoint operator

Let $a = a^* \in \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$. By the spectral theorem, there is a projection-valued measure $E_a : \mathcal{B}(\sigma(a)) \to \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$ such that $a = \int_{\sigma(a)} \lambda \, dE_a(\lambda)$.

**Theorem 7.1. [Sketched — via spectral theorem and bounded Borel functional calculus.]** *The vN algebra generated by $a$ is*
$$
W^*(a) := \{a\}'' = \{f(a) : f \in L^\infty(\sigma(a), E_a)\}
$$
*— the algebra of bounded measurable functions of $a$, viewed as multiplication operators via the spectral theorem.*

**Proof sketch.** The argument has two layers — a *continuous* functional calculus from C\*-theory, then a *bounded Borel* functional calculus from the spectral theorem.

*Step 1 (continuous functional calculus).* Polynomials in $a$ are in $W^*(a)$ by definition. By Stone–Weierstrass, polynomials are uniformly (norm-) dense in $C(\sigma(a))$, so the continuous functional calculus gives $\{f(a) : f \in C(\sigma(a))\} \subset W^*(a)$.

*Step 2 (extending to bounded Borel functions via the spectral theorem).* The spectral theorem for self-adjoint $a$ produces a projection-valued measure $E_a$ on Borel subsets of $\sigma(a)$, and a *bounded Borel functional calculus* $f \mapsto f(a) := \int f\, dE_a$ defined for every bounded Borel $f \in L^\infty(\sigma(a), E_a)$. The output $f(a)$ is in the *double commutant* of $a$: for any $T \in \{a\}'$, one has $T E_a(B) = E_a(B) T$ for every Borel $B$ (a standard consequence of the spectral theorem), hence $T f(a) = f(a) T$ for every bounded Borel $f$. So $f(a) \in \{a\}'' = W^*(a)$.

This gives $\{f(a) : f \in L^\infty(\sigma(a), E_a)\} \subset W^*(a)$. *Note:* the inclusion does not go through "polynomials SOT-approximate bounded Borel functions"; that statement is true but follows from the spectral theorem rather than from Stone–Weierstrass directly.

*Reverse inclusion.* Every element of $W^*(a)$ commutes with $\{a\}'$. By the same spectral-theorem reasoning, every operator commuting with $\{a\}'$ on $\mathcal{H}$ is a bounded Borel function of $a$. Hence $W^*(a) \subset \{f(a) : f \in L^\infty(\sigma(a), E_a)\}$. $\square$

This is a structural fact: the vN algebra of a single self-adjoint operator is *commutative*, and is $L^\infty$ of the spectrum. The non-commutativity of vN algebras enters only when we have *several* non-commuting operators.

**Corollary 7.2. [Stated only — follows from spectral multiplicity theory; refs: Bratteli–Robinson Vol. I §2.4.4.]** *$W^*(a)$ is a maximal abelian vN algebra iff the spectrum of $a$ is multiplicity-free* (i.e., the spectral measure $E_a$ has no non-trivial "multiplicity").

**Example 7.3.** For $a = $ multiplication by $x$ on $L^2[0,1]$: spectrum is $[0,1]$, multiplicity-free, so $W^*(a) = L^\infty[0,1]$ acting as multiplication. This is the canonical model of a maximal abelian vN algebra.

**Example 7.4 (Multiplicity issue).** For $a = \mathrm{diag}(0, 0, 1) \in M_3(\mathbb{C})$: $\sigma(a) = \{0, 1\}$, with multiplicities 2 and 1. $W^*(a)$ consists of operators of the form $\mathrm{diag}(\alpha, \alpha, \beta)$ — only 2-dimensional — which is *not* maximal abelian in $M_3(\mathbb{C})$. The maximal abelian extension is $\mathrm{diag}(\alpha, \beta, \gamma)$, which is 3-dimensional and corresponds to a "multiplicity-resolving" version of $a$.

## 8. Generation by sets of operators

**Definition 8.1.** Given a self-adjoint subset $S \subset \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$, the vN algebra *generated* by $S$ is $S''$.

**Lemma 8.2. [Sketched — direct from Theorem 4.1.]** *$S''$ equals the WOT-closure of the unital \*-algebra generated by $S$.*

(*Reason:* $S''$ is WOT-closed and contains the algebra generated by $S$. Conversely, the WOT-closure of any unital \*-subalgebra equals its double commutant, by Theorem 4.1.)

**Example 8.3 (Two non-commuting projections).** Let $p, q \in \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$ be two projections. The algebra $\{p, q\}''$ is non-commutative in general — and its structure is surprisingly rich. In finite dimensions ($\mathcal{H} = \mathbb{C}^n$), this leads to the *Halmos two-projection theorem*: $\{p, q\}''$ decomposes the Hilbert space into 1- and 2-dimensional invariant subspaces, with $p, q$ acting as $\pm 1$ on the 1-dimensional pieces and as orthogonal projections in $\mathbb{C}^2$ on the 2-dimensional pieces. The 2-dimensional pieces have a continuous parameter (the "angle" between $p$ and $q$).

The Halmos two-projection structure will appear in the GNS representation of group vN algebras and in the type II$_1$ structure of free-group factors. We will come back to it indirectly.

## 9. What to take away

1. **vN algebras are C\*-algebras with a built-in representation.** Closing in WOT/SOT instead of norm gives access to spectral projections, and via the bicommutant theorem, to the full algebraic-topological duality.

2. **The bicommutant theorem.** Algebraic ($\mathcal{M} = \mathcal{M}''$) ↔ topological (WOT/SOT/σ-weak-closed). This is the central structural result: it allows us to construct vN algebras as $S''$ for any self-adjoint $S$, and to verify the vN-algebra property either algebraically or topologically.

3. **Factors are the building blocks.** Every vN algebra decomposes as a direct integral of factors over its center; the type classification (next week) classifies factors.

4. **The predual encodes normal states.** The predual $\mathcal{M}_*$ is canonically defined; normal states are physically meaningful, non-normal states are pathological.

5. **Spectral projections are in $\mathcal{M}$.** This is the property that makes vN algebras useful for measurement theory and entropy calculations.

6. **The vN algebra of a single operator is commutative**, and equals $L^\infty$ of its spectrum. Non-commutativity enters only with several non-commuting generators.

## 10. Looking ahead: Week 3

Next week we classify factors into types I, II, and III using the *order structure on projections*. Type I is the "ordinary" case — every type I factor is $\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})$ for some Hilbert space $\mathcal{K}$. Type II admits a trace and houses the hyperfinite II$_1$ factor and group vN algebras of icc groups. Type III admits no trace at all — and is, for reasons we will work out in Block C, the type relevant to QFT. The fact that QFT lives in type III is what motivates the rest of the course.
