## 1. Why the free scalar?

The free scalar field is the simplest relativistic QFT and the only one in which every algebraic question we want to ask has an explicit answer. We use it as the **testbed** for everything in Blocks C and D. Three properties make it the right starting point:

1. **Fock-space realization.** The field acts on a known Hilbert space — Fock space over the one-particle space $L^2(\mathbb{R}^d, d\mu)$ — and one can write every operator in terms of creation and annihilation operators in modes.

2. **Weyl-algebra structure.** The bounded local algebras $\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{O})$ are generated by *exponentiated* smeared fields — the Weyl operators $W(f) = e^{i\phi(f)}$ — and the multiplicative law between them is encoded in a *symplectic* form $\sigma$ on test functions. The entire algebraic content of the free scalar is in this one bilinear form.

3. **Computable modular theory.** For wedge regions, the modular operator is known explicitly via Bisognano–Wichmann (Week 10). For double cones in 2D conformal theories, Hislop–Longo extends the result. For general bounded regions in 4D, the modular operator is not explicit but its existence and type are controlled (Week 12). Everywhere a research-level question is asked about the free scalar, we have tools to answer it.

The cost is that the free scalar misses interactions and gauge structure. The benefit is that *everything* in the rest of the course can be tested on it as a controlled limit: the Bisognano–Wichmann boost identification, the type III$_1$ structure of local algebras (under suitable hypotheses), the crossed product (Block D), the Bell–CHSH violation between Rindler wedges (Week 11), and the Sem II free-field mini-calculations for Witten 2022 and Ahmad–Jefferson.

## 2. The free scalar field

We work on $(d+1)$-dimensional Minkowski space $\mathbb{R}^{1,d}$ with signature $(-,+,\ldots,+)$, metric $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(-1, +1, \ldots, +1)$, and coordinates $x^\mu = (x^0, x^1, \ldots, x^d)$. Mass parameter $m \ge 0$.

### 2.1 Mode expansion

**Definition 2.1.** The **free scalar field of mass $m$** is the operator-valued distribution
$$
\phi(x) = \int \frac{d^d k}{(2\pi)^d \sqrt{2\omega_k}}\left[a_k\,e^{-i\omega_k x^0 + i\vec k\cdot\vec x} + a_k^\dagger\,e^{+i\omega_k x^0 - i\vec k\cdot\vec x}\right], \quad \omega_k = \sqrt{|\vec k|^2 + m^2},
$$
acting on Fock space $\mathcal{F} = \bigoplus_{n=0}^\infty \mathrm{Sym}^n(L^2(\mathbb{R}^d))$, with $[a_k, a_{k'}^\dagger] = (2\pi)^d\, \delta^d(\vec k - \vec k\,')$ and a vacuum $\Omega_0 = |0\rangle$ satisfying $a_k|0\rangle = 0$.

This is a *distribution* in $x$, not a genuine operator — squaring it gives ultraviolet divergences (because the integrand $1/\omega_k$ does not give a normalizable mode at large $|\vec k|$). The operators we will actually use are **smeared** versions.

### 2.2 Smeared fields

**Definition 2.2.** For a real-valued test function $f \in \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{1,d})$ (Schwartz, i.e., rapidly decreasing smooth), the **smeared field** is
$$
\phi(f) := \int d^{d+1}x\, f(x)\, \phi(x).
$$
$\phi(f)$ is a (densely defined) self-adjoint operator on $\mathcal{F}$ for real $f$, with the vacuum in its domain.

**Lemma 2.3. [Stated only — refs: Reed–Simon Vol. II §X.7.]** *The smeared fields $\phi(f)$ are essentially self-adjoint on the dense subspace of Fock-space vectors with finitely many particles and Schwartz wavefunctions. The closure (still denoted $\phi(f)$) is self-adjoint.*

In computational practice, we work with the dense domain of "nice" vectors and only worry about closures and domain technicalities at the very end. The Weyl-operator construction below sidesteps the domain issue entirely by exponentiating.

### 2.3 Properties of $\phi(f)$

The smeared field is:
- **linear in $f$:** $\phi(\alpha f + \beta g) = \alpha\,\phi(f) + \beta\,\phi(g)$ for $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$ (with conjugation rules for complex $f$).
- **self-adjoint:** $\phi(f)^* = \phi(f)$ for real $f$.
- **continuous in $f$:** $\phi(f_n)\Omega \to \phi(f)\Omega$ in norm when $f_n \to f$ in Schwartz topology.
- **Klein–Gordon:** $\phi(\Box f - m^2 f) = 0$ as an operator identity (the equation of motion lives in the test-function space).

## 3. Two-point function and symplectic form

### 3.1 The Wightman function

**Definition 3.1.** The **Wightman two-point function** is
$$
W(f, g) := \langle 0 | \phi(f)\, \phi(g) | 0\rangle.
$$

By explicit computation in Fock space (use the mode expansion and $\langle 0|a_k a_{k'}^\dagger|0\rangle = (2\pi)^d\delta^d(\vec k - \vec k\,')$):
$$
W(f, g) = \int \frac{d^d k}{(2\pi)^d 2\omega_k}\, \tilde f(\omega_k, -\vec k)^*\, \tilde g(\omega_k, -\vec k),
$$
where $\tilde f(p) = \int d^{d+1}x\,f(x)\,e^{ip\cdot x}$ is the spacetime Fourier transform. This is a *positive sesquilinear form* on the complex test-function space: $W(f, f) = \int \frac{d^dk}{(2\pi)^d 2\omega_k}|\tilde f(\omega_k, -\vec k)|^2 \ge 0$.

### 3.2 The symplectic form

**Definition 3.2 (Convention).** Define the **symplectic form** $\sigma(f, g)$ on real test functions by
$$
[\phi(f), \phi(g)] = i\,\sigma(f, g)\,\cdot\,\mathbf{1},
$$
where the commutator is a $c$-number on Fock space. Equivalently, $\sigma(f, g) = 2\,\mathrm{Im}\,W(f, g)$. Concretely,
$$
\sigma(f, g) = \int d^{d+1}x\, d^{d+1}y\, f(x)\, \Delta(x-y)\, g(y),
$$
where $\Delta(x-y) := -i\,\langle 0|[\phi(x), \phi(y)]|0\rangle$ is the **Pauli–Jordan distribution** — supported on the light cone, antisymmetric, and vanishing for spacelike separation. The symplectic form is real, bilinear, antisymmetric in $(f, g)$, and **vanishes when $\mathrm{supp}(f)$ and $\mathrm{supp}(g)$ are spacelike separated**.

This is the Bratteli–Robinson convention. With it, the Weyl relation below takes the form $W(f) W(g) = e^{-i\sigma(f,g)/2} W(f + g)$; with the alternative convention $\sigma' = \sigma/2$ the relation reads $W(f) W(g) = e^{-i\sigma'(f,g)} W(f + g)$. Pick one and stay with it; we use the B-R convention throughout.

### 3.3 Why this symplectic form?

The Pauli-Jordan distribution $\Delta(x-y)$ is **the** locality-encoding object of the free scalar field. It tells us:

- **Spacelike vanishing** $\Rightarrow$ commutativity of $\phi(x)$ and $\phi(y)$ at spacelike separation;
- **Light-cone support** $\Rightarrow$ disturbances propagate causally;
- **Antisymmetry** $\Rightarrow$ the algebra has a symplectic (not Riemannian) structure.

In the abstract Weyl-algebra construction below, all the geometric information about the free scalar field is encoded in $\sigma$.

> **Physical picture.** The symplectic form is not a quantum object at all — it is the *classical phase space structure* of the field, in covariant clothing. Recall that the space of real solutions of the Klein–Gordon equation carries the (covariant phase space) symplectic form $\sigma(\varphi_1, \varphi_2) = \int_\Sigma (\varphi_1 \partial_\mu \varphi_2 - \varphi_2 \partial_\mu \varphi_1)\, d\Sigma^\mu$, independent of the Cauchy slice $\Sigma$ by the equations of motion. A test function $f$ determines a solution $\Delta f$ (the Pauli–Jordan propagator applied to $f$), and under this map the smearing form $\sigma(f,g)$ of Definition 3.2 *is* the classical symplectic form evaluated on the corresponding solutions. So the quantization recipe is exactly canonical: take the classical phase space $(V, \sigma)$, impose $[\phi(f), \phi(g)] = i\sigma(f,g)$ — Poisson brackets $\to$ commutators — and exponentiate. Everything relativistic (causal propagation, spacelike commutativity) is inherited from the support properties of the classical propagator. The quantum theory adds exactly one ingredient beyond $(V,\sigma)$: the choice of *state* (§7), which is where $\hbar$, particles, and temperature enter.

### 3.4 Example: 2D massless scalar

In $1+1$ dimensions with $m = 0$, the Pauli–Jordan distribution is
$$
\Delta(x - y) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{sgn}(x^0 - y^0)\,\Theta\!\left(-(x-y)^2\right),
$$
i.e., $-\tfrac{1}{2}$ inside the future light cone of $y$, $+\tfrac{1}{2}$ inside the past light cone of $y$, and zero outside. (We use $(x-y)^2 := -(x^0-y^0)^2 + (x^1-y^1)^2$, so $-(x-y)^2 > 0$ is the timelike region.)

**Verifying spacelike vanishing.** For $x, y$ with $(x-y)^2 > 0$ (spacelike separated), $\Theta(-(x-y)^2) = 0$, so $\Delta(x-y) = 0$. ✓

**Smearing.** For two real Gaussian bumps $f_R, f_L$ supported in the right and left Rindler wedges (spacelike separated), $\sigma(f_R, f_L) = 0$. For $f_R, g_R$ both in the right wedge, $\sigma(f_R, g_R)$ is generally nonzero and is the input to the Weyl-algebra structure below.

### 3.5 Example: 4D massless scalar

In $3+1$ dimensions with $m = 0$,
$$
W(x - y) = -\frac{1}{4\pi^2}\,\frac{1}{(x - y)^2 - i\epsilon(x^0 - y^0)}, \qquad \Delta(x-y) = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\,\mathrm{sgn}(x^0 - y^0)\,\delta((x-y)^2),
$$
where the $i\epsilon$ prescription handles the light-cone singularity. The Pauli-Jordan in 4D is a delta function on the light cone, with sign determined by time ordering. Smearing against test functions $f, g$ gives finite $\sigma(f, g)$ that are integrals over the light cone of the difference variable.

### 3.6 Example: 2D massive scalar

In $1+1$ dimensions with $m > 0$,
$$
\Delta(x - y) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{sgn}(x^0 - y^0)\,J_0\!\left(m\sqrt{-(x-y)^2}\right)\,\Theta(-(x-y)^2),
$$
where $J_0$ is the Bessel function. Inside the light cone, the Pauli-Jordan oscillates with amplitude $\sim 1/\sqrt{m|x-y|}$ at large $m|x-y|$. The structure is the same: spacelike vanishing, light-cone support is generalized to "inside the light cone with oscillatory decay."

## 4. Weyl operators

The smeared fields $\phi(f)$ are unbounded. For modular theory we want bounded generators of the local algebras. The standard trick: exponentiate.

### 4.1 Definition

**Definition 4.1.** For real $f \in \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{1,d})$, the **Weyl operator** is
$$
W(f) := \exp\!\left(i\,\phi(f)\right).
$$
$W(f)$ is unitary (since $\phi(f)$ is self-adjoint), hence bounded with $\|W(f)\| = 1$.

The Weyl operators are bounded, so they generate honest C*-algebras; products of them generate von Neumann algebras after WOT-closure (the closure that produces local-algebra structure).

> **Physical picture.** Exponentiation is more than a domain trick. (1) *Operationally*, $W(f)$ is a **displacement operator**: acting on the vacuum it produces a coherent state — the quantum state closest to the classical field configuration $\Delta f$. The Weyl generators are "switch on a classical source $f$, coherently," which is the most physical family of local operations available in a free theory. (2) *Informationally*, an unbounded $\phi(f)$ is not a single measurable quantity but a whole spectral family; the unitary $W(\alpha f) = e^{i\alpha\phi(f)}$ packages all of it in bounded form, and the parameter family $\alpha \mapsto W(\alpha f)$ recovers $\phi(f)$ by Stone's theorem when needed. (3) *Structurally*, boundedness is what allows the C\*/von Neumann machinery of Weeks 1–7 — states, GNS, modular theory — to be applied verbatim: those theorems are theorems about bounded operators. The price of exponentiation is that the linear structure of the field is hidden inside the phase $e^{-i\sigma/2}$ of the Weyl relation; the bargain is that the entire theory becomes a C\*-algebra with the phase as its only relation.

### 4.2 The Weyl relation

**Theorem 4.2 (Weyl relation). [Proved.]** *For real $f, g \in \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{1,d})$,*
$$
W(f)\, W(g) = e^{-i\sigma(f,g)/2}\, W(f + g).
$$

**Proof.** On the analytic-vector subspace, the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff identity gives
$$
e^{X} e^{Y} = e^{X + Y + \tfrac{1}{2}[X, Y] + \cdots},
$$
where the higher commutators vanish when $[X, Y]$ is central. Apply with $X = i\phi(f)$, $Y = i\phi(g)$. Then $[X, Y] = -[\phi(f), \phi(g)] = -i\sigma(f, g)\cdot\mathbf{1}$, so $\tfrac{1}{2}[X, Y] = -\tfrac{i}{2}\sigma(f, g)\cdot\mathbf{1}$, and
$$
e^{i\phi(f)}\, e^{i\phi(g)} = e^{i\phi(f) + i\phi(g) - i\sigma(f, g)/2} = e^{-i\sigma(f, g)/2}\, e^{i\phi(f+g)},
$$
using $\phi(f) + \phi(g) = \phi(f + g)$ on the analytic-vector subspace. Extending by closure gives the operator identity on Fock space. $\square$

### 4.3 Consequences

1. $W(0) = 1$, $W(f)^{-1} = W(-f) = W(f)^*$.

2. **Conjugation by phase:**
$$
W(f)\,W(g)\,W(f)^* = e^{-i\sigma(f, g)}\, W(g).
$$
Weyl operators conjugate one another by phase factors determined by $\sigma$.

3. **Spacelike commutativity:** if $\sigma(f, g) = 0$ (in particular if $f, g$ have spacelike-separated support), then $W(f) W(g) = W(f + g) = W(g) W(f)$, so **$W(f)$ and $W(g)$ commute**.

4. **Continuity:** $W(f_n) \to W(f)$ in WOT when $f_n \to f$ in Schwartz topology (Stone's theorem for one-parameter unitary groups, applied to the family $\alpha \mapsto W(\alpha f)$).

5. **Vacuum expectation (Gaussian):** for real $f$,
$$
\omega_0(W(f)) := \langle 0|W(f)|0\rangle = e^{-W(f, f)/2}.
$$
This is the Gaussian-state character formula. It can be derived directly from the mode expansion (expand $\phi(f) = \phi_+(f) + \phi_-(f)$ into positive- and negative-frequency parts, where $\phi_-$ contains only $a_k^\dagger$ and $\phi_+$ contains only $a_k$; then $W(f) = e^{i\phi_+(f)}e^{i\phi_-(f)}e^{-W(f,f)/2}$; vacuum sandwich gives the result).

The Weyl operators are the fundamental bounded generators of the free-field algebra. Their algebraic skeleton is captured entirely by the symplectic form $\sigma$.

### 4.4 The CCR algebra

Abstractly, fixing a real symplectic vector space $(V, \sigma)$, the **canonical commutation relation (CCR) algebra** $\mathcal{W}(V, \sigma)$ is the unital C*-algebra generated by symbols $W(f)$, $f \in V$, with relations
$$
W(f)^* = W(-f), \qquad W(f)\,W(g) = e^{-i\sigma(f,g)/2}\,W(f + g).
$$
For the free scalar field on Minkowski space, $V$ is the space of real-valued Schwartz functions modulo the Klein–Gordon equation (i.e., test functions that integrate to zero against on-shell solutions), and $\sigma$ is the Pauli–Jordan form.

**Theorem 4.3 (Slawny). [Stated only — refs: Bratteli–Robinson Vol. II §5.2.3; Manuceau 1968.]** *Up to isomorphism, there is a unique simple C*-algebra $\mathcal{W}(V, \sigma)$ with the Weyl relations, provided $\sigma$ is non-degenerate on $V$.*

This uniqueness is the C*-algebraic statement that every quasi-free representation of the free scalar is fundamentally the same algebra, differing only in the choice of state.

### 4.5 The vacuum state and its GNS representation

The **Fock vacuum state** on $\mathcal{W}(V, \sigma)$ is the Gaussian state with covariance given by the symmetric part of the Wightman function:
$$
\omega_0(W(f)) = \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{Re}\,W(f, f)\right).
$$
The GNS representation of this state is the Fock-space representation of §2: the Hilbert space is $\mathcal{F}$, the cyclic vector is $|0\rangle$, and the Weyl operators act as $e^{i\phi(f)}$. This is the **Fock vacuum representation** of the free scalar field. (The von Neumann closure is then the algebra of all bounded functions of the field on Fock space; it is type I when restricted to a single mode, but the global field algebra is more delicate.)

Different choices of quasi-free state give different (generally inequivalent) GNS representations — this is the **failure of Stone–von Neumann** in infinite dimensions, and it is why algebra-level statements about the free scalar are more robust than Hilbert-space-level statements.

## 5. Local algebras

The Weyl algebra has a *net structure* indexed by spacetime regions.

### 5.1 Definition

**Definition 5.1.** For an open region $\mathcal{O} \subset \mathbb{R}^{1,d}$, the **local Weyl algebra** is
$$
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{O}) := \mathrm{C}^*\!\left(W(f)\; :\; \mathrm{supp}(f) \subset \mathcal{O}\right) \subset \mathcal{W}(V, \sigma),
$$
i.e., the C*-subalgebra generated by Weyl operators with test-function support in $\mathcal{O}$. The **local von Neumann algebra** (in the Fock-space representation) is the WOT-closure:
$$
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{O}) := \mathcal{W}(\mathcal{O})''.
$$

### 5.2 Properties

The local-net data $\{\mathcal{O} \mapsto \mathcal{A}(\mathcal{O})\}$ satisfies (most of) the Haag–Kastler axioms (Week 9):

- **Isotony:** $\mathcal{O}_1 \subset \mathcal{O}_2 \Rightarrow \mathcal{A}(\mathcal{O}_1) \subset \mathcal{A}(\mathcal{O}_2)$. *[Proved here, immediate from the definition.]*

- **Locality:** $\mathcal{O}_1, \mathcal{O}_2$ spacelike $\Rightarrow$ $[\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{O}_1), \mathcal{A}(\mathcal{O}_2)] = 0$. *[Proved here.]* This follows from the Weyl relation and the spacelike vanishing of $\sigma$: $W(f) W(g) = W(g) W(f)$ when $\sigma(f, g) = 0$, and commuting generators give commuting algebras. The argument extends from generators to the full algebra by linearity, multiplication, and WOT-continuity.

- **Covariance:** the Poincaré group acts on the test-function space ($f \mapsto f \circ \Lambda^{-1}$ for $\Lambda \in \mathcal{P}_+^\uparrow$) and hence on the Weyl algebra by automorphisms, mapping $\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{O})$ to $\mathcal{A}(\Lambda \mathcal{O})$. *[Sketched — uses the unitary implementer of $\Lambda$ on Fock space, which exists because the vacuum is Poincaré-invariant and the Weyl algebra is generated by smeared fields.]*

- **Vacuum invariance:** $U(\Lambda)|0\rangle = |0\rangle$ for $\Lambda \in \mathcal{P}_+^\uparrow$, by construction of the Fock vacuum.

- **Spectral condition:** $P^0 \ge 0$ on Fock space (since particle number is non-negative and energies $\omega_k \ge 0$).

These five properties are the **Haag–Kastler axioms** for a local net of algebras; they are introduced systematically next week.

### 5.3 Locality made explicit

For Weyl operators $W(f), W(g)$ with $\mathrm{supp}(f) \subset \mathcal{O}_1$, $\mathrm{supp}(g) \subset \mathcal{O}_2$, and $\mathcal{O}_1, \mathcal{O}_2$ spacelike separated:

$$
\sigma(f, g) = \int \int f(x) \Delta(x-y) g(y)\, d^{d+1}x\,d^{d+1}y = 0
$$

(because $\Delta(x-y) = 0$ for spacelike $x-y$, and every pair $x \in \mathrm{supp}(f), y \in \mathrm{supp}(g)$ is spacelike). Hence

$$
W(f) W(g) = e^{-i\cdot 0/2} W(f+g) = W(f+g) = W(g) W(f).
$$

So $W(f) W(g)^{-1} W(f)^{-1} W(g) = W(f) W(-g) W(-f) W(g) = e^{-i\sigma(f,g) - i\sigma(-f,-g) + \ldots} W(0) = 1$ — the group commutator is trivial, hence the operator commutator is trivial.

## 6. Worked example: 2D massless scalar in two wedges

Take $d = 1$ (so $(d+1) = 2$), $m = 0$. The right and left **Rindler wedges** are
$$
W_R = \{x: x^1 > |x^0|\}, \qquad W_L = \{x: x^1 < -|x^0|\}.
$$
These are spacelike separated, and each is invariant under the boost $x^0 \mapsto \cosh(\eta) x^0 + \sinh(\eta) x^1$, $x^1 \mapsto \sinh(\eta) x^0 + \cosh(\eta) x^1$ (in $W_R$ for $\eta \in \mathbb{R}$, and similarly $W_L$ for the reversed boost).

### 6.1 Test functions

Choose two real Gaussian bumps $f_R, g_R$ supported in $W_R$:
$$
f_R(x) = c_1\, e^{-((x^0 - t_1)^2 + (x^1 - s_1)^2)/\sigma^2}\,\mathbf{1}_{W_R}, \qquad s_1 > |t_1| + 3\sigma,
$$
and similarly $g_R$ centered at $(t_2, s_2)$, $s_2 > |t_2| + 3\sigma$. The cutoffs ensure the supports lie strictly inside $W_R$ (the Gaussian decay is fast enough that the contribution from outside is negligible).

### 6.2 Pauli–Jordan integration

Using the 2D massless formula from §3.4,
$$
\sigma(f_R, g_R) = \int d^2x\, d^2y\, f_R(x)\,\left[-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{sgn}(x^0 - y^0)\Theta(-(x-y)^2)\right]\, g_R(y).
$$
For widely separated Gaussian centers $(t_1, s_1)$ and $(t_2, s_2)$ both in $W_R$, the integral is dominated by the region where $x \in \mathrm{supp}(f_R)$ and $y \in \mathrm{supp}(g_R)$ are timelike-related (inside each other's light cones). The integral equals a finite nonzero number, computable in closed form via Gaussian integrals over error-function-bounded regions:
$$
\sigma(f_R, g_R) \;\sim\; -\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{sgn}(t_1 - t_2)\,\pi\sigma^2\,e^{-((t_1-t_2)^2 + (s_1-s_2)^2)/(2\sigma^2)} \cdot \mathrm{erf-factor},
$$
schematically. The sign is determined by time ordering; the magnitude is suppressed exponentially by the spatial and temporal separations.

### 6.3 Weyl algebra in the wedge

The von Neumann algebra
$$
\mathcal{A}(W_R) = \{W(f) : \mathrm{supp}(f) \subset W_R\}''
$$
contains $W(f_R), W(g_R)$ and all their finite products. The Weyl relation gives
$$
W(f_R)\,W(g_R) = e^{-i\sigma(f_R, g_R)/2}\,W(f_R + g_R),
$$
which is again in $\mathcal{A}(W_R)$ because $f_R + g_R$ has support in $W_R$.

### 6.4 Locality across the wedges

For $f_R$ supported in $W_R$ and $g_L$ supported in $W_L$, every pair $x \in \mathrm{supp}(f_R), y \in \mathrm{supp}(g_L)$ has $x^1 > 0$ and $y^1 < 0$, with $(x-y)^2 > 0$ (spacelike). So $\Delta(x-y) = 0$ on this region, and $\sigma(f_R, g_L) = 0$. Hence $W(f_R)\, W(g_L) = W(g_L)\, W(f_R)$, and $[\mathcal{A}(W_R), \mathcal{A}(W_L)] = 0$.

### 6.5 Why this calculation matters

This calculation is the workhorse for everything that follows in Block C:

- Week 9 (Reeh–Schlieder): the vacuum is cyclic-separating for $\mathcal{A}(W_R)$, with the dense subspace built from polynomials in $\phi(f_R)$.
- Week 10 (Bisognano–Wichmann): the modular flow on $\mathcal{A}(W_R)$ acts on Weyl operators by $W(f_R) \to W(f_R \circ \Lambda^{\mathrm{boost}}(-2\pi t))$ — a geometric boost on test functions.
- Week 11 (Bell–CHSH): dichotomic observables $\cos(\alpha\phi(f_R))$, $\cos(\alpha\phi(g_L))$ in the two wedges saturate the Tsirelson bound. The group's research program is built on this construction.

The free 2D massless scalar is the testbed where all of these are explicitly computable.

## 7. Quasi-free states beyond the vacuum

The choice of state on $\mathcal{W}(V, \sigma)$ fixes the GNS representation; the vacuum is by no means the only physically relevant choice.

### 7.1 Definition

**Definition 7.1.** A state $\omega$ on $\mathcal{W}(V, \sigma)$ is **quasi-free** (or **Gaussian**) if it has the form
$$
\omega(W(f)) = \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\, S(f, f)\right)
$$
for some positive sesquilinear form $S$ on $V_{\mathbb{C}}$ with $\mathrm{Im}\, S(f, g) = \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma(f, g)$, i.e., $S$ is a positive completion of the symplectic structure.

The Fock vacuum corresponds to $S(f, g) = \mathrm{Re}\,W(f, g) + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma(f, g)$ — the standard Wightman function.

> **Physical picture.** A quasi-free state is "a definition of *particle*." The algebra $(V, \sigma)$ knows nothing about particles — it is classical phase space with a commutator. Choosing the covariance $S$ amounts to choosing how to split field oscillations into positive and negative frequency (mathematically: a compatible complex structure on $V$), and that split is exactly what defines creation versus annihilation, hence the vacuum, hence "a particle." Different legitimate splits — inertial time versus Rindler time, Minkowski modes versus Boulware or Hartle–Hawking modes in a black-hole background — give different quasi-free states, generally in *inequivalent* GNS representations. The Bogoliubov coefficients between two splits measure how many "particles" of one definition the other definition's vacuum contains: this is the entire content of the Unruh and Hawking effects at the kinematical level. The algebraic moral, which Slawny's theorem makes precise: *the field is intrinsic; the particle is a property of the state.*

### 7.2 Examples of other quasi-free states

- **Thermal states.** For a free scalar in a fixed inertial frame with Hamiltonian $H$, the **KMS state** at inverse temperature $\beta$ is quasi-free, with covariance
$$
S_\beta(f, f) = \int \frac{d^dk}{(2\pi)^d 2\omega_k}\,\coth(\beta\omega_k/2)\,|\tilde f(\omega_k, -\vec k)|^2.
$$
At $\beta \to \infty$, $\coth \to 1$ and we recover the vacuum.

- **The Rindler vacuum.** As we'll see in Week 10, the "vacuum" defined by accelerated observers (annihilated by Rindler-positive-frequency modes) is a different quasi-free state from the Minkowski vacuum. The Bogoliubov transformation between them is the Unruh effect.

- **Bisognano–Wichmann (advance preview).** The Minkowski vacuum *restricted* to the wedge algebra $\mathcal{A}(W_R)$ behaves like a thermal-like state for the boost generator at temperature $T_U = 1/(2\pi)$ in Rindler units. This is the algebraic content of the Unruh effect, and it is the input to Week 10's central computation.

### 7.3 The space of quasi-free states is convex

Every convex combination of quasi-free states is again a state (linear); generically it is *not* quasi-free, but it has a quasi-free description on a richer GNS representation. The set of quasi-free states is the structural arena of "free-field physics."

## 8. What to take away

- **Stated only:** the free scalar field is an operator-valued distribution on Fock space; smeared fields $\phi(f)$ are densely defined self-adjoint operators (Reed–Simon Vol. II §X.7).
- **Proved:** the bounded Weyl operators $W(f) = e^{i\phi(f)}$ satisfy the Weyl relation $W(f) W(g) = e^{-i\sigma(f,g)/2}\, W(f+g)$, with $\sigma$ the Pauli–Jordan symplectic form (B-R convention).
- **Proved:** local algebras at spacelike separation commute. Locality at the algebraic level follows from the spacelike vanishing of $\sigma$.
- **Computed here:** the 2D massless Pauli–Jordan distribution and the symplectic form between two Gaussian bumps in a wedge.
- **Physical interpretation:** the Weyl algebra is the *only* algebraic data of the free scalar; the choice of state (vacuum, thermal, Rindler vacuum, etc.) determines the representation, but the algebra is intrinsic.

## 9. Looking ahead

Week 9 introduces the **Reeh–Schlieder theorem**: in any Wightman QFT (free or interacting), the vacuum is cyclic and separating for every local algebra $\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{O})$ of a non-empty open region with non-empty open complement. This is what licenses applying Tomita–Takesaki to local algebras: every local algebra has a canonical modular operator with respect to the vacuum. Reeh–Schlieder is **the** bridge between abstract modular theory and QFT.

## 10. Problem set

**Core problems.**

**1. The Weyl relation in a wedge.** Pick two real Gaussian bumps $f, g$ supported in the right Rindler wedge of the 2D massless free scalar (use the prescription of §6.1). Compute $\sigma(f, g)$ approximately for two specific centers $(t_1, s_1), (t_2, s_2)$ in $W_R$, and verify the Weyl relation $W(f) W(g) = e^{-i\sigma(f, g)/2} W(f + g)$ by acting on the vacuum (compare two-point functions of both sides).

**2. Spacelike commutativity.** Let $\mathcal{O}_1, \mathcal{O}_2$ be open regions with $\mathcal{O}_1 \subset \mathcal{O}_2'$ (i.e., $\mathcal{O}_1$ is spacelike to $\mathcal{O}_2$). Show that $\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{O}_1)$ and $\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{O}_2)$ commute. (*Hint:* show the generators commute via the Weyl relation, then extend to the WOT-closures.)

**3. Continuity in the test function.** Let $f_n \to f$ in $\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{1,d})$. Show that $\phi(f_n)|0\rangle \to \phi(f)|0\rangle$ in norm. Conclude that $W(f_n) \to W(f)$ in WOT.

**4. The vacuum is Gaussian.** Verify $\omega_0(W(f)) = e^{-W(f, f)/2}$ for real $f$, using the Fock-space realization. (*Hint:* compute $\langle 0|e^{i\phi(f)}|0\rangle$ by expanding $\phi(f) = \phi_+ + \phi_-$ into positive- and negative-frequency parts, where $\phi_+$ annihilates the vacuum on the right and $\phi_-$ creates from the left. Use the BCH identity $e^{X+Y} = e^X e^Y e^{-[X,Y]/2}$ for $X = i\phi_-(f), Y = i\phi_+(f)$.)

**5. Symplectic form for displaced Gaussians.** For two Gaussian bumps centered at $(t_1, s_1)$ and $(t_2, s_2)$ in 2D massless, with widths $\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = \sigma$, write $\sigma(f_1, f_2)$ as a 2D integral and identify when it vanishes (spacelike separation) and when it is maximal.

**6. Pauli–Jordan in 4D massless.** Verify the 4D massless Pauli–Jordan formula $\Delta(x-y) = (1/2\pi)\,\mathrm{sgn}(x^0 - y^0)\,\delta((x-y)^2)$ from the Fock-space mode expansion. (*Hint:* compute $-i\langle 0|[\phi(x), \phi(y)]|0\rangle$ in mode integrals; the delta-on-light-cone result follows from the $\omega_k = |\vec k|$ Lorentz-invariant measure.)

**Starred problems.**

**7\*. The Weyl algebra is simple.** Sketch a proof that the Weyl C*-algebra $\mathcal{W}(V, \sigma)$ is simple when $\sigma$ is non-degenerate. (*Hint:* show that any nontrivial two-sided ideal would contain a multiple of the identity, contradicting unitality. Use the conjugation property $W(f)W(g)W(f)^* = e^{-i\sigma(f,g)}W(g)$ and the non-degeneracy of $\sigma$.)

**8\*. Boosted Weyl operators in the wedge.** In the 2D massless free scalar, let $\Lambda_\eta$ be a boost by rapidity $\eta$ in the $(x^0, x^1)$ plane. Show that under $\Lambda_\eta$, $W(f) \mapsto W(f \circ \Lambda_\eta^{-1})$, and that this maps $\mathcal{A}(W_R)$ into itself for every $\eta \in \mathbb{R}$.

**9\*. Type-I regularization.** Cut off the free scalar in momentum space: keep only modes $|k| < \Lambda$. Show that the resulting algebra is type I, with the vacuum state having a density matrix on a finite-mode Fock-cone. Explain why the type-I structure breaks down in the $\Lambda \to \infty$ limit. *(This previews why bounded-region local algebras become type III$_1$ in the unregularized theory.)*

**10\*. Massive vs. massless 2D.** Repeat the 2D Pauli–Jordan computation for $m > 0$. Show that the support of $\Delta(x-y)$ is still inside the light cone (the field commutator is supported timelike-or-null even for massive fields), but the coefficients involve Bessel functions $J_0(m\sqrt{-(x-y)^2})$.

**11\*. Inequivalent representations.** Take the free scalar in 2D massless on the half-line $\{x^1 > 0\}$ at $x^0 = 0$. Consider two states: the Minkowski vacuum restricted to this half-line, and the Rindler vacuum (lowest-energy state with respect to the boost generator). Show that the GNS representations of these two states are *not* unitarily equivalent. (*Hint:* this is one form of the Stone–von Neumann failure.)
