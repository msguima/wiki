# Week 15 — Consolidation: Everything Was Symmetry All Along

> *This week rereads fourteen weeks of lattice calculations in one language and introduces no new technique. In every week the decisive objects were supported on closed submanifolds: the seams of Kramers and Wannier, Wegner's loops and flipped sheets, the vortex lines that Wilson loops become in Polyakov's dual photon, 't Hooft's twisted sheets and flux sectors, and the residual symmetry of $\mathbb{Z}_N$ gauge theory with charge-$q$ matter. Each was either a topological operator or an object detected by one, and the phases of the semester were told apart by how those operators were realized. Semester II calls this generalized symmetry, following Gaiotto, Kapustin, Seiberg and Willett. We state their definitions precisely, translate one Semester I model into their language from end to end as a template, collect every duality, criterion and realization of the semester in tables that point back to their derivations, and assign the take-home final.*

### How to use this chapter

- **In class:** state the definitions of §2, checking each clause against the form-degree table of [[courses/generalized-symmetries-course/conventions|conventions]] §6; derive at the board the translation exercise of §3 in its six steps, with the linking identity (3.1) and the ground-state count (3.3) in full; then walk through Tables 1–4 with the course map of Figure 2, asking for the week and the mechanism behind each row. Problems 1–3 are the classroom core. The last half hour presents the take-home final (§14), whose package carries the paper and its diagnostic map.
- **For self-study:** §§8–12 and Problems 4⋆–5⋆. The one calculation to do alone is Problem 4⋆, the translation of the four-dimensional ℤ₂ gauge theory, with its eight ground states on the spatial three-torus.
- **Instructor checkpoint:** two errors recur. The first is to call the 't Hooft loop the generator of the magnetic symmetry: for a $U(1)$ gauge field in $d=4$ the generator is the surface operator $\exp(i\alpha\oint_\Sigma F/2\pi)$ on a closed surface, and the 't Hooft line is the charged object it detects by linking (F3 adds the Villain subtlety). The second is to read a perimeter law as spontaneous breaking after charged matter has broken the symmetry explicitly: the criterion presupposes an exact symmetry, and with fundamental matter neither the Wilson loop nor the Polyakov loop decides anything ([[week-13-fradkin-shenker-gauge-higgs|Week 13]], [[week-14-fradkin-shenker-order-parameters|Week 14]]).

## 1. Motivation: what the semester was about

In Landau's classification a phase is labelled by a group acting on local fields and by an order parameter, a local operator whose expectation value vanishes in the symmetric phase. Semester I tested that picture on lattice models and saw it fail in a definite sequence. In the two-dimensional XY model the continuous symmetry cannot break ([[week-01-compact-variables-xy-model|Week 1]] §3.5), and the BKT transition of [[week-04-bkt-kramers-wannier-disorder|Week 4]] is the unbinding of vortices, diagnosed by the jump of the stiffness. In Wegner's gauge theory ([[week-05-wegner-z2-gauge-theory|Week 5]]) Elitzur's theorem forbids every local order parameter, and the phases differ in the asymptotics of Wilson loops. Compact QED₃ ([[week-09-compact-qed3-monopole-plasma|Week 9]], [[week-10-polyakov-mass-gap-area-law|Week 10]]) has no transition at all and confines at every coupling, because monopoles gap the photon. Four-dimensional compact QED ([[week-11-monopole-condensation-4d|Week 11]]) has a Coulomb phase and a confining phase, separated by the proliferation of monopole loops. With fundamental matter the Higgs and confining regimes form a single phase ([[week-13-fradkin-shenker-gauge-higgs|Week 13]]), while with charge-$q$ matter in a $\mathbb{Z}_N$ theory a residual $\mathbb{Z}_{\gcd(N,q)}$ separates phases ([[week-14-fradkin-shenker-order-parameters|Week 14]]). In each case the diagnostic that worked was an operator on a closed loop or surface, and its behavior was controlled by another operator that could be deformed without changing any correlation function. The thesis of the week is the sentence that organizes these results,
$$
\textit{a phase is characterized by how its topological operators are realized and by which extended operators they detect,}
$$
and our task is to make it precise enough that every Semester I result becomes a statement about one such operator. Landau's order parameters are the case in which the charged operator is a point. What his picture could not describe in Semester I involved charged operators of higher dimension, symmetries broken explicitly by dynamical defects, or no exact symmetry at all.

## 2. The GKSW definitions, stated precisely [Stated — refs: GKSW §§1–5; [[courses/generalized-symmetries-course/conventions|conventions]] §6. Model case in §3.]

We state the definitions in the form Semester II uses, with $d$ the Euclidean spacetime dimension and $D=d-1$ the spatial dimension of a Hamiltonian slice ([[courses/generalized-symmetries-course/conventions|conventions]], course-local override), and we translate each clause into the lattice language of Semester I.

**Topological operator.** An operator $U(M)$ on a closed submanifold $M$ is topological when its correlation functions do not change under deformations of $M$ that avoid the other insertions: $\langle U(M)\,\mathcal O_1\cdots\mathcal O_k\rangle=\langle U(M')\,\mathcal O_1\cdots\mathcal O_k\rangle$ whenever $M'-M$ bounds a region that meets no $\mathcal O_i$. On the lattice $M$ is a cycle of Λ or $\Lambda^*$, and topological invariance was proved the same way every time: the deformation $M\to M+\partial R$ is undone by a change of summation variables supported in $R$ (Week 4 §4.1 for the Ising seam, Week 5 §8.1 for the flipped sheet, Week 11 §5.1 for the twisted sheet, Week 14 §3.1 with matter).

**$p$-form symmetry** ([[higher-form-symmetries]]). A $p$-form symmetry with group $G$ is a family of topological operators $U_g(M)$ on closed $(d-p-1)$-dimensional submanifolds, with $U_g(M)U_h(M)=U_{gh}(M)$. Its charged objects $W(C)$ live on closed $p$-dimensional $C$, and the charge is measured by linking,
$$
\big\langle U_g(M)\,W(C)\cdots\big\rangle=\chi(g)^{{\rm Link}(M,C)}\,\big\langle W(C)\cdots\big\rangle,\tag{2.1}
$$
where $\chi:G\to U(1)$ is the character that defines the charge of $W$, ${\rm Link}(M,C)$ is the linking number of the two cycles in $d$ dimensions, and $M$ bounds a region that contains no other insertion. For $p=0$ the operators have codimension one and act on local operators, the ordinary notion of symmetry. For $p\ge1$ the group is abelian, because operators of codimension two or more can be moved around one another. In a Hamiltonian slice the operator at fixed time lives on a closed $(D-p)$-dimensional submanifold of space, commutes with $H$, and depends only on its homology class once Gauss's law holds. For a $U(1)$ gauge field in $d$ dimensions the electric symmetry is 1-form and the magnetic one $(d-3)$-form: 0-form with local monopole operators in $d=3$, 1-form with 't Hooft lines in $d=4$.

**Explicit breaking and emergence.** An element $g$ is explicitly broken when no topological $U_g$ exists at any coupling. For the 1-form symmetries of Semester I the mechanism was a dynamical object on which the charged lines can end: matter for Wilson lines (Week 14 §3.1), monopoles for 't Hooft lines (Week 11 §5.2). For the 0-form winding symmetry in $d=2$ and the magnetic $U(1)^{(0)}$ in $d=3$ the charged local operators themselves enter the action through the fugacity, as $2y\cos\chi$ and $2\zeta\cos\sigma$ (Week 3 §6; Week 9 §3; [[courses/generalized-symmetries-course/conventions|conventions]] §6). A symmetry is emergent when it is explicitly broken but the breaking is irrelevant at long distances in one phase; it organizes that phase and carries no exact consequence.

**Spontaneous breaking for $p\ge1$.** For an exact $p$-form symmetry, spontaneous breaking means that its charged operators obey a perimeter law, $\langle W(C)\rangle\,e^{\mu|C|}\to{\rm const}\ne0$ for large $C$ with a local counterterm $\mu|C|$ on $C$ (F2), and the symmetry is unbroken when they decay faster, by an area law for $p=1$ ([[courses/generalized-symmetries-course/conventions|conventions]] §6). On a spatial manifold with noncontractible cycles the broken phase has degenerate ground states, because the symmetry operators on noncontractible cycles and the charged operators on the cycles that cross them realize a clock–shift algebra on the ground space ([[week-02-lattice-cell-complex-cochains|Week 2]] §8; §3.4 and F1 below). The higher-form Coleman–Mermin–Wagner theorem forbids the spontaneous breaking of a continuous $q$-form symmetry for $q\ge d-2$; discrete symmetries break in lower dimensions ([[courses/generalized-symmetries-course/conventions|conventions]] §6).

**Twists and backgrounds.** A symmetry operator on a noncontractible cycle is a twisted boundary condition: the XY twist α across a seam multiplies each current sector by $e^{i\alpha w_1}$ ([[week-03-villain-form-xy-duality|Week 3]] F3), and in 't Hooft's analysis on the three-torus the temporal twists are the operators of $\mathbb{Z}_N^{(1)}$ inserted in the trace, while the spatial twists label magnetic flux sectors that no operator creates (Week 14 §6.2). Coupling to backgrounds, summing over them (gauging), and the rule that gauging $\mathbb{Z}_N^{(q)}$ in $d$ dimensions produces a dual $\mathbb{Z}_N^{(d-q-2)}$ ([[courses/generalized-symmetries-course/conventions|conventions]] §6) are defined in Semester II Week 4; Semester I uses them only as forward references.

These clauses make the final well posed: each question it asks about a symmetry is a question about one of them.

## 3. The translation exercise: Wegner's ℤ₂ gauge theory

This section is the template for the translation problems of §13 (Problems 1, 2 and 4⋆), and the symmetry parts of the final use the steps it names. A translation answers six questions in order: (i) the exact topological operators, with their group and degree; (ii) the charged objects and the linking action; (iii) the realization in each phase; (iv) the sectors and ground states on a spatial torus; (v) the open versions of the symmetry operators and what sits at their boundaries; (vi) what is exact and what is emergent, and where Semester II attaches anomaly and defect language. Consider the model of [[week-05-wegner-z2-gauge-theory|Week 5]], $S=-\beta\sum_P\sigma_P$ with $\sigma\in C^1(\Lambda,\mathbb{Z}_2)$, in $d=3$ unless stated otherwise, together with its $2+1$ Hamiltonian $H=-\Gamma\sum_\ell\sigma^x_\ell-K\sum_PB_P$ and Gauss's law $A_v\equiv\prod_{\ell\ni v}\sigma^x_\ell=1$ ([[courses/generalized-symmetries-course/conventions|conventions]] §4).

### 3.1 The topological operator [Proved.]

Let $\tilde\Sigma=\partial\tilde V$ be a closed dual $(d-2)$-chain, $F$ the set of plaquettes dual to its cells, and $U(\tilde\Sigma)$ the operation that reverses the coupling, $\beta\to-\beta$, on $F$. Let $\lambda\in C^1(\Lambda,\mathbb{Z}_2)$ equal $-1$ on the links dual to the cells of $\tilde V$ and $+1$ elsewhere. A plaquette has $(d\lambda)_P=-1$ exactly when an odd number of its links is dual to cells of $\tilde V$, which happens exactly when $P\in F$ (Week 5 §§7.3, 8.1). The substitution $\sigma=\sigma'\lambda$ gives $\sigma_P=\sigma'_P(d\lambda)_P$, so the reversed weight on $F$ and the ordinary weight elsewhere both become $e^{\beta\sigma'_P}$, while a Wilson loop becomes $W(C;\sigma)=(-1)^{\#(C\cap\tilde V)}W(C;\sigma')$, where $\#(C\cap\tilde V)$, the number of links of $C$ dual to cells of $\tilde V$, is the linking number ${\rm Link}(C,\tilde\Sigma)$ mod 2. Therefore, at every β,
$$
\boxed{\;\langle U(\tilde\Sigma)\rangle=1,\qquad \langle W(C)\,U(\tilde\Sigma)\rangle=(-1)^{{\rm Link}(C,\tilde\Sigma)}\,\langle W(C)\rangle,\qquad U(\tilde\Sigma)^2=1,\;}\tag{3.1}
$$
where the last relation holds because reversing twice restores the weight. Two sheets that differ by the boundary of a region avoiding $C$ give the same correlators, so $U$ is topological, with group $\mathbb{Z}_2$, on closed supports of dimension $d-2=d-p-1$: a 1-form symmetry. Its support is a dual loop in $d=3$ (Figure 1a) and a dual surface in $d=4$. In $d=3$ the closed sheet is also the worldline of a static vison, since reversing β on $F$ makes the frustrated value of $\sigma_P$ the preferred one; (3.1) says that this worldline is invisible except through its Aharonov–Bohm phase $-1$ on the Wilson loops it links. In the Hamiltonian slice, $U(\tilde\gamma)=\prod\sigma^x_\ell$ over the links crossed by a closed dual curve $\tilde\gamma$ of space is the parity of the electric flux through $\tilde\gamma$; it commutes with $H$, and Gauss's law deforms it, since moving $\tilde\gamma$ across a site $v$ multiplies it by $A_v=1$.

### 3.2 The charged objects

The charged objects are the Wilson loops $W(C)=\prod_{\ell\in C}\sigma_\ell$, one-dimensional as the degree requires, with the charge $\chi(-1)=-1$ of (3.1). Every gauge-invariant local operator is a function of plaquette variables (Week 5 §3), and a closed sheet can always be deformed away from its support, after which (3.1) shows that the operator is neutral. The model has no other exact internal symmetry ([[courses/generalized-symmetries-course/conventions|conventions]] §6, "Lattice versus TQFT"). That is the precise content of "no local order parameter": the only charged operators are lines.

### 3.3 The realization in each phase [Stated — refs: Week 5 §§4–8, where each result is Controlled or Computed.]

The criterion of §2 reads the realization from the asymptotics of $W(C)$. At strong coupling the surface expansion gives an area law with $\sigma=-\ln t-2(d-2)t^4+O(t^6)$, $t=\tanh\beta$ (Week 5 §§4.1–4.2); at weak coupling the dilute gas of small vison loops gives a perimeter law with $\mu=2e^{-4(d-1)\beta}$ (Week 5 §5); and in $d=3$ the duality $\tanh\beta=e^{-2K^*}$ locates the transition at $\beta_c=0.76141$, with the confined phase mapped to the ordered Ising phase (Week 5 §7.1). Therefore $\mathbb{Z}_2^{(1)}$ is unbroken in the confined phase $\beta<\beta_c$ and spontaneously broken in the deconfined phase $\beta>\beta_c$. In $d=2$ it is unbroken at every β, where the area law is exact (Week 5 §6), and in $d=4$ it breaks at the self-dual point $\beta_{\rm sd}=0.44069$, where the transition is first order [Stated — refs, Week 5 §8.3]. The dual Ising $\mathbb{Z}_2^{(0)}$ is realized the other way round: broken in the confined phase, where the visons condense, $\langle\mu\mu\rangle=1-4t^6+O(t^{10})$, and unbroken in the deconfined phase, where the vison is a gapped particle of mass $2\beta-4e^{-2\beta}+O(e^{-4\beta})$ (Week 5 §7.3). The duality exchanges a broken and an unbroken symmetry, which is the lattice face of the gauging rule of §2 at $q=0$, $d=3$: summing the Ising model over its twisted sectors (Week 5 F6) gauges its $\mathbb{Z}_2^{(0)}$ and produces the $\mathbb{Z}_2^{(d-q-2)}=\mathbb{Z}_2^{(1)}$ of (3.1) [forward reference: Semester II Week 4].

### 3.4 The torus: sectors and ground states [Computed at the soluble points; the splitting Sketched.]

Take the Hamiltonian on an $L\times L$ spatial torus with cycles $C_1$ and $C_2$, Wilson loops $W_i=\prod_{\ell\in C_i}\sigma^z_\ell$, and symmetry operators $U_i=\prod\sigma^x_\ell$ over the links crossed by a closed dual curve $\tilde\gamma_i$ that crosses $C_i$ once and $C_j$, $j\ne i$, not at all (Figure 1c). Since $\sigma^x_\ell$ and $\sigma^z_\ell$ anticommute on the one common link and commute elsewhere,
$$
W_iU_j=(-1)^{\delta_{ij}}\,U_jW_i,\qquad [U_i,H]=0\quad\text{at every }(\Gamma,K),\tag{3.2}
$$
where the second relation holds because every plaquette contains zero or two of the links crossed by $\tilde\gamma_i$. The four sectors labelled by the eigenvalues of $U_1$ and $U_2$ never mix ([[week-07-kogut-susskind-hamiltonian|Week 7]] §7.4).

*The deconfined soluble point, $\Gamma=0$.* The ground states have $B_P=1$ on every plaquette, so their configurations are flat, $\sigma\in Z^1(T^2,\mathbb{Z}_2)$. The Gauss-law projector sums each configuration over its gauge orbit, so the physical ground states correspond one to one to the orbits of flat configurations, and two flat configurations lie on one orbit exactly when they have the same holonomies $(W_1,W_2)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\;{\rm GSD}(T^2)=\big|Z^1/B^1\big|=\big|H^1(T^2,\mathbb{Z}_2)\big|=4,\;}\tag{3.3}
$$
where $H^1(T^2,\mathbb{Z}_2)=\mathbb{Z}_2^2$ is Week 2 §4.3; this is (7.1) of Week 14 at $N=2$, $q\equiv0$. The count is forced by (3.2): $(W_1,U_1)$ and $(W_2,U_2)$ are two commuting $\mathbb{Z}_2$ clock–shift pairs, whose irreducible representation has dimension $2^2$ (Week 2 §8). In the holonomy basis the $W_i$ are diagonal and play the role of the order parameter, and the $U_i$ permute the four states as the global flip permutes the two ferromagnetic states of an Ising chain.

*The confined soluble point, $K=0$.* $H=-\Gamma\sum_\ell\sigma^x_\ell$ has the unique ground state with every $\sigma^x_\ell=+1$, which obeys Gauss's law and has $U_i=1$. The operator $W_i$ creates a loop of electric flux along $C_i$ at energy $2\Gamma L$, so the other sectors cost an energy proportional to $L$, and the degeneracy is 1.

*Away from the soluble points.* The four sectors stay exact by (3.2), and their energies can differ only through terms of the effective Hamiltonian on the ground space that distinguish the eigenvalues of $U_i$, the lowest of which is $U_i$ itself, a product of $L$ operators $\sigma^x$. The splitting therefore appears first at order $(\Gamma/K)^L$ and is exponentially small in $L$ (Week 14 §7.2; we omit the prefactor, as there). In the Euclidean path integral the same four states are the ratio $|H^1(T^3,\mathbb{Z}_2)|/|H^0(T^3,\mathbb{Z}_2)|=4$ of Week 5 F6.

### 3.5 Open sheets and their boundaries [Stated — refs: Week 5 §§7.3, 8.2.]

If $\tilde\Sigma$ has a boundary, the reversal cannot be undone: at each cube dual to a cell of $\partial\tilde\Sigma$ the effective field strength violates the Bianchi identity (Week 5 §8.2). The open symmetry operator is invisible along its interior, by the substitution of §3.1, and physical at its boundary, which is the worldvolume of a $\mathbb{Z}_2$ magnetic source of dimension $d-3$. In $d=3$ the boundary is a pair of points and the operator is the vison pair, $\langle\mu(\tilde x)\mu(\tilde y)\rangle_\beta=\langle s_{\tilde x}s_{\tilde y}\rangle_{K^*}$ (Week 5 §7.3; Figure 1b): the dual Ising spin is the endpoint of an open symmetry operator, as the Kadanoff–Ceva μ is the endpoint of an open Ising seam (Week 4 §4.1). In $d=4$ the boundary is a dual loop and the operator is the 't Hooft loop, with a perimeter law in the confined phase and an area law in the deconfined one; the self-duality exchanges it with $W$ (Week 5 §§8.2–8.3). The vison is local only together with its invisible string, and on a torus only pairs are defined (Week 5 F7).

### 3.6 What is exact, what is emergent, and where Semester II attaches [Stated — forward references.]

The electric $\mathbb{Z}_2^{(1)}$ of (3.1) is exact at every β. In the deconfined phase the visons are gapped, so large vison worldlines are rare and the Wilson loops become topological at long distances: they generate an emergent magnetic $\mathbb{Z}_2^{(1)}$ acting on vison worldlines, the magnetic entry of the $\mathbb{Z}_N$ BF row of [[courses/generalized-symmetries-course/conventions|conventions]] §6 with $d-p-1=1$. At $\Gamma=0$ it is exact on the ground space, where (3.2) says that the generators of each symmetry are charged under the other. Semester II reads this in three ways. (i) *Anomaly:* the algebra (3.2) is the finite-volume face of the mixed 't Hooft anomaly of the electric and magnetic 1-form symmetries, which forces the degeneracy (3.3) wherever both are present (Block 1, Week 2, and Block 2). (ii) *Defects:* the open sheet of §3.5 is a symmetry defect with a boundary, and the vison is a non-genuine local operator at its end; the sector sum of Week 5 F6 is the gauging of the Ising $\mathbb{Z}_2^{(0)}$ (Semester II Week 4). (iii) *Condensation:* summing the operators $U$ over the 1-cycles of a surface in the deconfined phase condenses visons on that surface, which Semester II Week 13 calls the higher gauging of $\mathbb{Z}_2^{(1)}$ and a condensation defect ([[condensation-defects]]), and which the group's research line reads as a [[julia-toulouse-mechanism|Julia–Toulouse]] condensation restricted to a submanifold. The deconfined phase itself is the $\mathbb{Z}_2$ [[topological-order]] of the [[toric-code]], whose soluble point is the $\Gamma=0$ Hamiltonian with Gauss's law imposed energetically (Week 7 §8). These six answers are the template: a translation (Problems 1, 2 and 4⋆ of §13) is complete when it answers (i)–(vi) with a derivation or a precise citation for each.

## 4. Duality is Poisson resummation

Every duality of the semester was the recipe of Week 2 §7.2: write the compact theory with an explicit integer (or $\mathbb{Z}_2$) cochain, resum it with the identities of [[courses/generalized-symmetries-course/conventions|conventions]] §3, integrate the original field to obtain a conservation constraint, and solve the constraint on the dual lattice by a dual potential plus winding sectors; the defects reappear only at a second resummation. ## 5. Criteria and their failures

The Landau paradigm failed at the places listed in §1, and at each a criterion built on an extended operator took its place. Table 2 lists the criteria, what each measures in the language of §2, and where each fails. The failures carry as much information as the successes: most happen where the symmetry that the criterion measures is explicitly broken or absent, and the others where an exact continuous symmetry cannot break at all, as in the two-dimensional XY model (Mermin–Wagner) and in three-dimensional Maxwell theory (its higher-form version).

## 8. Subtleties and fine print

**F1 — Finite versus infinite volume.** In finite volume no symmetry of any degree is spontaneously broken. On the $L\times L$ torus the ground states of §3.4 are exact eigenstates of $U_1$ and $U_2$, and the four sectors are split at order $(\Gamma/K)^L$; broken symmetry is the statement that the splitting vanishes as $L\to\infty$ and that the states of definite holonomy, in which $\langle W_i\rangle\ne0$, are the ones that cluster. The limits are taken in the order of Week 5 F1, the volume first and the loops afterwards. For a 0-form symmetry the splitting of the ordered doublet requires an operator on all of space, $L^D$ sites, and for a 1-form symmetry an operator on a closed curve, $L^{D-1}$ links (Week 14 §7.2); in both cases it is exponentially small in a power of $L$, and for the 1-form symmetry the number of sectors depends on the topology of space, $r^{b_1}$ (Week 14 §7.1). In the Euclidean path integral on a torus, (3.1) holds for sheets that bound. On a noncontractible cycle $\langle U\rangle$ differs from 1, since the operator is then a twisted boundary condition that weights the flux sectors (Week 3 F3; Problem 1(d) of the take-home final in `generalized-symmetries/assessments/final.md`), as the reversed seam of the Ising model gives $\langle\eta\rangle=Z_{\rm AP}/Z_{\rm P}$ (Problem 1); Problem 5⋆ computes it for Wegner's model.

**F2 — The perimeter-law normalization.** The coefficient μ of a perimeter law is a self-energy of the probe: it depends on the lattice action and on the corners of $C$, nothing universal attaches to its value (Week 5 F3), and in the continuum it diverges as $|C|/a_{\rm lat}$. The SSB criterion therefore refers to $\langle W(C)\rangle e^{\mu|C|}$ with a counterterm local on $C$, which removes perimeter and corner terms and can never remove an area term, since a counterterm local on $C$ grows at most like $|C|$. The invariant tools are the Creutz ratio, which cancels perimeter, corner and constant terms exactly (Week 5 F3; [[week-06-wilson-action-strong-coupling|Week 6]] §6), and, with matter, the Fredenhagen–Marcu ratio, whose square root cancels the self-energy of an open line (Week 13 F5). "Perimeter law" in Tables 2 and 3 means "no area term".

**F3 — The Villain 't Hooft loop.** In the Villain theory with dynamical monopoles the integer twisted sheet bounded by $\tilde C$ is a relabeling of the summed integers, so the integer 't Hooft loop equals 1 identically and the magnetic symmetry is explicitly broken in every phase (Week 11 §5.2). The entries of Tables 2–4 that give 't Hooft loops of $U(1)$ theories a perimeter or an area law refer to a static monopole probe with its Coulomb field, to the emergent magnetic symmetry of the Coulomb phase, or to the monopole-free theory, in which $dn=0$ is imposed and the loop becomes the Wilson loop of the dual photon (Semester II Week 12). In $\mathbb{Z}_2$ gauge theory the Bianchi identity holds identically, and the 't Hooft loop of Week 5 §8.2 is a well-defined line operator in $d=4$, mutually non-local with the Wilson loop (Week 5 §8.3). It is charged under the electric $\mathbb{Z}_2^{(1)}$ of the self-dual description, which has no generator in the original variables, since the product of $\sigma_P$ over a closed surface is identically 1 (Week 5 F6); the magnetic symmetry of the $\mathbb{Z}_2$ BF row is a 2-form symmetry with surface charged objects (Problem 3).

**F4 — Explicit versus spontaneous breaking.** The SSB criterion presupposes an exact symmetry. With charged dynamical matter the would-be symmetry operator acquires local factors on the region it bounds and a perimeter law in its own size (Week 14 §3.1; Week 13 Problem 4⋆), Wilson loops obey a perimeter law in every phase (Week 13 §2.4), and nothing follows about the realization of any symmetry. With charge-$q$ matter in $\mathbb{Z}_N$ the exact part is ${\rm Ann}(\langle q\rangle)\cong\mathbb{Z}_{\gcd(N,q)}^{(1)}$ (Week 14 §3), and the criterion applies to it alone. The Polyakov loop is likewise an order parameter only for the part of the center that the matter leaves exact: all of $\mathbb{Z}_N$ for center-neutral matter, nothing for fundamental matter, where $\langle L\rangle\ne0$ at every coupling (Week 14 §4.3). Emergent symmetries (the magnetic ones of the Coulomb and deconfined phases, the winding symmetry of the BKT phase) organize long-distance physics and carry no exact theorem.

**F5 — Local observables still see the transitions.** A phase transition without a local order parameter is still visible to local observables: the plaquette energy of Wegner's model is singular at $\beta_c$, with the specific-heat singularity of the 3d Ising model (Week 5 §11), and every transition of Tables 2 and 3 is a free-energy singularity. What is absent is a local operator that vanishes by symmetry in one phase and not in the other.

**F6 — Which dimension.** Every degree in this note uses $d$ for the spacetime dimension. A $p$-form symmetry has operators of dimension $d-p-1$ in spacetime and $D-p$ in a spatial slice, and charged objects of dimension $p$. The magnetic symmetry of a $U(1)$ gauge field is $(d-3)$-form, a 0-form symmetry with local monopole operators in $d=3$ and a 1-form symmetry with 't Hooft lines in $d=4$. Evaluating a rule written for the spatial dimension at the spacetime dimension is the origin of erratum E1 ([[courses/generalized-symmetries-course/conventions|conventions]], course-local override), and every symmetry statement of the final is checked against the master table.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"Generalized symmetry is a reformulation with no new content."** It is tempting because every result of Semester I was derived without the language, and the tables of this week relabel those results. The language has consequences that no single calculation of the semester stated. The degeneracy (3.3) follows from the algebra (3.2) of symmetry operators and charged lines alone, so it holds wherever that algebra holds on the ground space, including points with no soluble limit. Two phases in which an exact symmetry is realized differently must be separated by a transition, which is why the $N=6$, $q=4$ diagram of Week 14 (its Figure 2) needs a transition line while the charge-1 diagram does not (Week 14 §3.5). The higher-form Coleman–Mermin–Wagner theorem constrains every three-dimensional model at once: no continuous 1-form symmetry breaks there ([[courses/generalized-symmetries-course/conventions|conventions]] §6). And with fundamental matter the absence of any exact symmetry is the invariant content of Fradkin–Shenker complementarity. Semester II adds 't Hooft anomalies, which constrain phases through the algebra of the operators without any dynamical calculation.
- **"The 't Hooft loop generates the magnetic 1-form symmetry."** It is tempting because both carry the adjective magnetic and the 't Hooft loop is built from a twisted sheet. The generator of the magnetic $U(1)^{(1)}$ of four-dimensional Maxwell theory is the surface operator $\exp(i\alpha\oint_\Sigma F/2\pi)$ on a closed 2-surface, and the 't Hooft line is its charged object, detected by linking. The twisted sheet on a closed surface generates the electric symmetry, and the 't Hooft loop is the boundary of an open one (Week 11 §§5.1–5.2; §3.5 for $\mathbb{Z}_2$). Its first appearances are Week 5 §8.2 for $\mathbb{Z}_2$ and Week 11 §5 for $U(1)$.
- **"The Polyakov loop is the order parameter of confinement whatever the matter."** It is tempting because in pure gauge theory the center symmetry forces $\langle L\rangle=0$ in the confined phase exactly. With fundamental matter the center symmetry is explicitly broken and $\langle L\rangle=h_1^{N_\tau}\ne0$ at every coupling, so the Polyakov loop separates nothing; it is an order parameter for center-neutral matter, and with charge-$q$ matter only the loops $L_e$ with $e\notin H$ remain order parameters: $\langle L_e\rangle$ vanishes identically in finite spatial volume and is nonzero in infinite volume only if the residual $\mathbb{Z}_r$ breaks spontaneously, which it can at high temperature (Week 14 §§4.1, 4.3).

## 10. Historical note

Gaiotto, Kapustin, Seiberg and Willett (2014) wrote in continuum language, with differential forms and background gauge fields. They defined a $q$-form symmetry by topological operators of codimension $q+1$, showed that the group is abelian for $q\ge1$, coupled the symmetries to $(q+1)$-form backgrounds, and discussed gauging, 't Hooft anomalies and spontaneous breaking, including the higher-form Coleman–Mermin–Wagner theorem; their examples include Maxwell theory and $\mathbb{Z}_N$ gauge theory, the continuum versions of our Weeks 5, 8–11 and 14. The ingredients were older, and Semester I read them in their original form: Wegner's loop correlations and duality (1971), Wilson's area-law criterion (1974), Polyakov's monopole plasma (1975–77), the duality of Banks, Myerson and Kogut (1977), and 't Hooft's classification of phases by electric and magnetic flux (1978), whose temporal twists are symmetry operators of the center 1-form symmetry. What the 2014 paper supplied was one definition that covers all of them and puts the loop criteria on the footing of Landau's.

## 11. What to take away

1. **A $p$-form symmetry is a family of topological operators on closed $(d-p-1)$-dimensional submanifolds that act by linking on $p$-dimensional charged objects** [Stated; model case Proved]. On the lattice topological invariance is always a change of variables; physically, a symmetry operator can be moved without being noticed, except by what it links.
2. **Wegner's theory, translated:** an exact electric $\mathbb{Z}_2^{(1)}$ with Wilson lines as charged objects, spontaneously broken in the deconfined phase with four ground states on $T^2$ forced by the clock–shift algebra (3.2), unbroken in the confined phase; the vison is the endpoint of an open symmetry operator [Proved and Computed].
3. **Every duality of the semester is Poisson (or $\mathbb{Z}_2$ Fourier) resummation followed by a constraint solved on the dual lattice,** with $\beta\to1/4\pi^2\beta$ in each $U(1)$ row. It exchanges a symmetry with a dual one whose charged objects are the defects, which break it explicitly.
4. **Each criterion measures the realization of one symmetry.** It fails where that symmetry is explicitly broken (Wilson and Polyakov loops with fundamental matter, 't Hooft loops with dynamical monopoles, local order parameters in gauge theories) and where a continuous symmetry cannot break at all (the two-dimensional XY model, three-dimensional Maxwell theory).
5. **Semester I's phases are classified by Table 3.** Where no exact symmetry survives, as with fundamental matter, no symmetry distinction between phases exists, and the deconfined phases are told apart by topological order, whose Semester I fingerprint is the degeneracy on the torus.

1. **The Ising translation.** The decisive step is the change of variables $s_x\to-s_x$ for $x\in R$, which restores every reversed bond, since exactly the bonds crossed by $\tilde\gamma$ have one end in $R$; it leaves the measure invariant and multiplies $s_xs_y$ by $(-1)^{[x\in R]+[y\in R]}$. The operator is topological, with group $\mathbb{Z}_2$, on a closed support of dimension $d-p-1=1$: a 0-form symmetry with the local charged operator $s_x$. It is broken for $K>K_c=0.440687$ and unbroken below; on the ring the states $|{\Uparrow}\rangle\pm|{\Downarrow}\rangle$ at $g\ll1$ split at order $g^N$, since all $N$ spins must flip, and the ground state at $g\gg1$ is unique. The open seam has the disorder operators μ at its ends, with $\langle\mu\mu\rangle_K=\langle ss\rangle_{K^*}$, so μ condenses in the disordered phase (Week 4 §§4.1–4.2). For (vi): the symmetry is exact, and the Kramers–Wannier map exchanges its broken and unbroken phases, which is the gauging of $\mathbb{Z}_2^{(0)}$ with the dual $\mathbb{Z}_2^{(d-0-2)}=\mathbb{Z}_2^{(0)}$ (forward reference); at $K_c$ it becomes the non-invertible line $D$ of Week 4 §4.4. Common failure mode: calling μ the symmetry operator, or expecting $\langle\eta\rangle$ to vanish in the broken phase. For bounding $\tilde\gamma$, $\langle\eta\rangle=1$ in every phase; on a torus a noncontractible $\tilde\gamma$ is the antiperiodic boundary condition of Week 4 §3.4, and $\langle\eta\rangle=Z_{\rm AP}/Z_{\rm P}$ tends to 0 in the ordered phase, where a domain wall of length $L$ is forced, and to 1 in the disordered one.
2. **Compact QED₄ at θ.** (a) The θ-modified Gauss law of Week 12 §4.1 makes $\nabla\cdot(E/e^2-\theta B/4\pi^2)$ the density of the integers $n_e$, so $\hat N(\partial V)$ is the total $n_e$ inside $V$, an integer by compactness (Week 12 §4.2), and $\exp(i\alpha\hat N)$ multiplies $W_q$ by $e^{iq\alpha{\rm Link}}$: an electric $U(1)^{(1)}$ with α defined mod $2\pi$. A dynamical monopole crossing Σ changes $\oint E/e^2$ by $\theta/2\pi$ and $\oint\theta B/4\pi^2$ by the same amount, so $\hat N$ is unchanged, while the flux of $E/e^2$ alone is not topological. The magnetic operator $\exp(i\alpha\oint_\Sigma F/2\pi)$ counts $n_m$ and is explicitly broken by the dynamical monopoles (Table 3; F3). (b) The line $(n_e,n_m)$ has charge $n_e$ under the electric symmetry, magnetic charge $n_m$ and physical charge $q_e=n_e+\theta n_m/2\pi$; at $\theta+2\pi$ the line labelled $(n_e,n_m)$ carries the charges of $(n_e+n_m,n_m)$ at θ, so the set of line labels returns, shifted ([[courses/generalized-symmetries-course/conventions|conventions]] §10); the dynamical spectrum returns only if dyons of every label are dynamical, and with monopoles alone the lattice θ-term is not $2\pi$-periodic. (c) With $w=(0,1)$ the pairing is $\langle v,w\rangle=n_e$, which confines $(1,0)$ and $(-1,1)$ and screens $(0,1)$; with $w=(-1,1)$ it is $n_e+n_m$, which confines $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ and screens $(-1,1)$ (Week 12 §7.3). The Wilson probe is confined on both sides; what changes at θ = π is which line with $n_m\ne0$ is screened, and at θ = π the two condensates tie and are exchanged by ${\rm CP}_{\theta=\pi}:(n_e,n_m)\to(-n_e-n_m,n_m)$ [Heuristic]. (d) In the pure theory the dynamical objects are monopoles with $n_e=0$, so the electric symmetry of (a) is exact at every θ and the magnetic one is explicitly broken; the oblique phases of Week 12 §7 belong to models with dynamical dyons of every $n_e$ (Week 12 §7.4 and its references), in which the electric symmetry is explicitly broken too and the phases are told apart by the pattern of (c). Semester II Week 6 reads the tie at θ = π as a mixed 't Hooft anomaly involving CP (forward reference; GKKS). Common failure mode: taking the flux of $E/e^2$ as the symmetry charge, which the dynamical monopoles change by θ/2π.
3. **The form-degree drill.** Compact scalar: shift, 0-form, charged $e^{iq\theta}$, operators of dimension $d-1$ in spacetime and $D$ in space, breaking allowed in $d=3,4$; winding, $(d-2)$-form, charged vortex lines in $d=3$ and vortex sheets in $d=4$, operators $\exp(i\alpha\oint d\theta/2\pi)$ on closed curves in both, breaking forbidden in both ($d-2\ge d-2$). $U(1)$ gauge field: electric 1-form, Wilson lines, operators of dimension $d-2$ (curves in $d=3$, surfaces in $d=4$), breaking forbidden in $d=3$ and allowed in $d=4$; magnetic $(d-3)$-form, local monopole operators and closed-surface operators in $d=3$, 't Hooft lines and closed-surface operators in $d=4$, breaking allowed in both. $\mathbb{Z}_2$ BF: electric 1-form with Wilson lines and operators of dimension $d-2$; magnetic $(d-2)$-form with charged objects of dimension $d-2$ (vison worldlines in $d=3$, surfaces in $d=4$) and the Wilson lines as operators; the theorem does not apply to discrete groups. Realized in Semester I: the $d=3$ compact scalar as the dual photon of Weeks 8–10, whose shift symmetry is the magnetic $U(1)^{(0)}$; the $U(1)$ gauge field in $d=3$ (Weeks 9–10) and $d=4$ (Week 11); and the $\mathbb{Z}_2$ rows as the deconfined limit of Wegner's model in $d=3$ and $d=4$ (Week 5; §3), whose lattice form keeps only the electric symmetry exact. Common failure mode: taking the operator dimension for the degree, or $(d-2)$ for the magnetic degree of a gauge field.
